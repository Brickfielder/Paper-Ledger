08:34Brain Injury after Cardiac ArrestTime recorded in notes

Overview

The session addressed neuromonitoring, prevention of secondary brain injury, human-tissue research after circulatory arrest, and emerging neuroprotective therapies after cardiac arrest. Speakers emphasized that cardiac-arrest survivors should generally be treated as patients with acute brain injury and that timing, mechanism, and individualized monitoring are central challenges.

Main Arguments

  • Neuromonitoring opportunities increase from the prehospital setting to the emergency department and intensive care unit, but early post-ROSC care remains a period of limited information about brain physiology.
  • Cerebral perfusion pressure or mean arterial pressure is important but is only an imperfect proxy for actual cerebral perfusion. Individual autoregulation and intracranial pressure may substantially alter the relationship.
  • Normalizing oxygenation, ventilation, temperature, and hemodynamics is a reasonable starting point, but guideline targets should not necessarily be viewed as definitive for every patient.
  • Neuroprotective trials should be mechanistically targeted: enroll patients with the relevant injury mechanism, treat during the active therapeutic window, and monitor target engagement.
  • Human tissue obtained through rapid post-mortem donation may provide a complementary platform for studying human neural circuits and testing mechanisms or therapies that cannot be studied directly in living patients.

Key Findings

  • A small Finnish prehospital study of 12 patients combined cerebral saturation and arterial pressure monitoring. Patterns differed between patients, suggesting that cerebral saturation may or may not track mean arterial pressure and that autoregulation may be impaired in some patients.
  • Retrospective cerebral oximetry analyses estimated patient-specific optimal mean arterial pressures, in examples ranging from approximately 55–60 to 95–100 mmHg; these calculations were not available in real time.
  • Evidence for cerebral saturation alone was described as inconsistent. Cerebral oximetry indices may provide information about autoregulation, but their clinical role remains uncertain.
  • Near-infrared spectroscopy is noninvasive, whereas brain-tissue oxygen, cerebral microdialysis, jugular venous oxygen saturation, and intracranial-pressure monitoring are more invasive and may provide continuous or regional information with important trade-offs.
  • Transcranial Doppler is feasible and reflects cerebral blood-flow velocity, but it is operator-dependent, limited by acoustic windows, and difficult to use continuously.
  • Bispectral-index patterns may help identify broad EEG patterns when full EEG monitoring is unavailable, although BIS is a calculated EEG measure originally developed for anesthetic depth monitoring.
  • Trials discussed by the second speaker generally did not show clear outcome benefit from mild hypercapnia or different blood-pressure targets, although subgroup effects and biological effects remain possible.
  • Early hypotension was associated with mortality in observational series; the speaker suggested avoiding early systolic blood pressure below approximately 100 mmHg while noting that later blood-pressure targets are more complex.
  • Preliminary human-donation research suggested that different neural functions cease sequentially during circulatory arrest: sensory evoked potentials first, followed by depth EEG, and later blood flow and tissue oxygen measurements.
  • Rapidly acquired post-mortem brain slices showed cellular staining and limited electrical activity in organotypic cultures, including activity reported hours to days after death. The speaker stressed that this was not life support or evidence of viability, but a research platform.
  • Proposed therapies included early cooling, inhaled xenon, hydrogen, and argon, as well as anti-inflammatory or immune-modulating approaches. The speakers repeatedly noted that delayed treatment may miss the relevant mechanism and therapeutic window.

Methods Or Evidence

  • Observational monitoring studies using cerebral oximetry, arterial pressure, intracranial pressure, transcranial Doppler, EEG/BIS, brain-tissue oxygen, microdialysis, and jugular venous oxygen saturation.
  • Retrospective calculations of cerebral oximetry indices and optimal mean arterial pressure.
  • Randomized trials and trial programs comparing oxygenation, carbon-dioxide, temperature, blood-pressure, and cooling strategies were discussed, though several study names and exact results were unclear in the transcript.
  • Human in-vivo monitoring during circulatory arrest followed by expedited autopsy and tissue banking. Research methods included electrophysiology, organotypic slice culture, patch clamping, immunohistochemistry, and planned spatial transcriptomics or proteomics.
  • Mechanism-based translation from human observational data to animal models and then to clinical trials, including studies of immune-cell populations and candidate neuroprotective treatments.

Practical Or Clinical Implications

  • Begin brain-focused care before ICU admission: avoid hypotension, hypoxia, hyperoxia, abnormal carbon dioxide levels, and fever while recognizing that exact individualized targets remain uncertain.
  • Use available neuromonitoring according to setting and expertise. Noninvasive monitoring may be more practical early, while invasive multimodal monitoring may be more informative but carries procedural burdens and may measure only local or regional physiology.
  • Interpret mean arterial pressure as a surrogate rather than a direct measurement of cerebral perfusion.
  • Consider full EEG when simplified BIS patterns suggest seizures, burst suppression, or other concerning activity.
  • Design and interpret neuroprotective trials around the timing of the biological injury mechanism; therapies aimed at early reperfusion injury may be ineffective if started hours later.
  • Combination therapies may be needed because post-cardiac-arrest injury involves ischemic, reperfusion, inflammatory, metabolic, and repair phases.

Limitations And Uncertainties

  • Several neuromonitoring findings came from small observational studies or retrospective calculations and are not validated as real-time treatment targets.
  • Cerebral saturation, intracranial pressure, Doppler velocity, and other measures differ in anatomical coverage and may not be directly comparable.
  • The commonly illustrated broad cerebral-autoregulation range was criticized as an oversimplification; the clinically relevant range may be narrower and patient-specific.
  • Evidence for specific oxygen, carbon-dioxide, blood-pressure, temperature, and sedation targets remains incomplete or neutral in the trials discussed.
  • The rapid-autopsy tissue platform involved preliminary, unpublished or early-stage work, with limited numbers of cases and uncertain relevance to resuscitated patients.
  • Human-donation experiments described non-shockable circulatory arrest and cannot automatically be generalized to successfully resuscitated cardiac-arrest patients.
  • Many trial names, numerical details, and technical terms were unclear or distorted in the transcript, so exact study identification and effect estimates should be checked against the original publications.

Audience Questions

  • Could the human-tissue findings change after reperfusion, and might injured neural tissue be placed in a state resembling hibernation rather than complete loss of function? The response was that reperfusion was not studied in the presented human tissue work, but the findings may indicate residual substrate for future investigation; the speaker expressed concern that overly aggressive reperfusion could worsen injury.
  • Should post-cardiac-arrest patients be cooled more rapidly or to much lower temperatures before gradual rewarming? This was raised as a speculative concept rather than a clinical recommendation.
  • Could artificial intelligence help integrate the complex neuromonitoring and tissue data? The question was noted in the discussion, but no substantive answer was recorded.
  • Is propofol harmful for the injured brain compared with midazolam, dexmedetomidine, or ketamine? The response favored studying possible subgroups rather than assuming a broad sedation comparison would show benefit, because many patients would recover regardless of sedative and others would not recover despite the choice.

Follow-up reading

  • The Finnish prehospital cerebral-saturation and arterial-pressure study described in the talk.
  • Studies of cerebral oximetry index and pressure-reactivity index after cardiac arrest.
  • Randomized trials discussed regarding mild hypercapnia, oxygen targets, blood-pressure targets, temperature management, and early cooling, including the named PRINCESS-2 program and the studies referred to as EXACT, BOX, COCA, Neuroprotect, and TTM-related programs; exact citations should be verified from the original sources.
  • The speaker’s prior publication on studying the human dying process as a model of cerebral ischemia.
  • Literature on human organ donation for research, rapid autopsy, organotypic brain-slice culture, and electrophysiological study of post-mortem human tissue.
  • Mechanism-based frameworks for post-cardiac-arrest brain injury and reverse translation from human immune-cell findings to neuroprotective therapies.
13:01Measuring What Matters in Cardiac Arrest SurvivorsTime recorded in notes

Overview

The session reviewed how recovery after cardiac arrest can be assessed and supported in clinical practice and research. Presenters emphasized that survival alone does not capture cognition, emotional well-being, fatigue, quality of life, everyday functioning, identity, relationships, return to work or education, and the experiences of co-survivors. The transcript identifies Robert Richardson, Marco Biondi, and Vicky Gerber among the presenters; other speaker names and the formal conference and talk titles were not provided.

Main Arguments

  • Recovery is dynamic, non-linear, and individualized rather than a single endpoint or score.
  • Existing cardiac arrest outcome measures may miss problems in everyday life, including fatigue, cognitive changes, emotional adjustment, confidence, identity, and return to meaningful roles.
  • Assessment should combine standardized questionnaires with clinical discussion and, where appropriate, functional or informant-based assessment.
  • Follow-up should extend beyond hospital discharge and may need to be longitudinal, because new difficulties and rehabilitation needs can emerge during later life transitions.
  • Structured, coordinated follow-up can connect hospitals and resources, but implementation requires organizational readiness, communication pathways, and attention to accessibility.
  • Measurement should be linked to care: if assessment does not change support or treatment, its value is questionable.

Key Findings

  • In the described multicenter Revival cohort, 65% of survivors scored below the stated MoCA threshold of 26 before discharge.
  • The cognitive domains described as most affected included selective memory and executive function. Survivors below the threshold before discharge had more than twice the risk of unfavorable cognitive outcomes at three months.
  • Before discharge, the presenters reported symptoms of anxiety in 25% of survivors, depression in 20%, and trauma reactions in 21%.
  • Survivors with these early psychopathology symptoms had more than four times higher odds of clinically significant anxiety, depression, or PTSD symptoms at three months.
  • In the Lombardia Revive pilot, 22 hospitals across seven provinces were involved. Approximately three quarters of eligible survivors were contacted, and approximately two thirds of those contacted completed the first assessment; the presentation characterized this as roughly half of eligible people overall.
  • Thirty-one potentially eligible survivors could not be contacted because contact details were unavailable.
  • Interviews sometimes identified fear, guilt, fatigue, and concentration difficulties that were not apparent from questionnaires alone. Conversely, low cognitive scores did not always correspond to difficulties in daily life.
  • Research discussed associations between trauma-related symptoms and poorer health outcomes, including readmission and fatigue, but the mechanisms were described as uncertain.

Methods Or Evidence

  • The previous year's workshop used small-group discussions to examine measurement tools, communication and support needs, co-survivors, trauma, PTSD, emotional recovery, quality of life, outcome measures, and follow-up systems.
  • The Revival cohort used the Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA), the Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale (HADS), and a measure described in the transcript as the Impact of Events scale to assess cognition, anxiety, depression, and trauma reactions.
  • The described Copenhagen pathway included screening before discharge, occupational-therapy involvement for low MoCA scores or concerns, psychological support for severe trauma reactions, discharge planning, rehabilitation plans, and referral for neuropsychological assessment when needed.
  • Follow-up in that pathway included a one-week telephone consultation and a two-month review of cognition, fatigue, mental health, everyday functioning, and return to work.
  • The Lombardia Revive program offered assessments at discharge and at one, three, six, and nine months, delivered in person, by telephone, or online. It used the MoCA, HADS, PTSD-related questionnaires, a generic quality-of-life measure, and semi-structured clinical interviews.
  • The research presentation discussed trauma, ecological validity of cognitive assessments, performance-based functional tests, informant reports, smartphone-based ecological assessments, and positive psychological factors such as existential well-being and post-traumatic growth.
  • The speakers also referred to planned or discussed use of fatigue and disability measures, but the transcript does not consistently identify all instruments by name.

Practical Or Clinical Implications

  • Establish a local multidisciplinary network and clarify responsibilities, referral routes, and points of contact before attempting a large regional system.
  • Provide survivors and families with basic information and signposting from the time of discharge.
  • Educate clinicians that cardiac arrest may result in brain injury and cognitive, emotional, or functional difficulties even when neurological outcome appears favorable.
  • Use questionnaires as a starting point rather than a substitute for discussion. The speakers suggested that no more than about half of a consultation should be devoted to questionnaires, as a rule of thumb.
  • Consider remote follow-up to extend specialist support to hospitals without local resources and to reduce travel, staffing, and clinic-space demands.
  • Screen for barriers to remote care, including cognitive impairment, limited digital skills, living alone, psychological distress, discomfort with technology, and the reduced ability to recognize acute distress remotely.
  • Measure change over time and individual progress, not only absolute scores or comparisons with pre-arrest functioning.
  • Include co-survivors and family perspectives in assessment and support planning.
  • Consider flexible or repeated assessment because depression, fatigue, and other needs may emerge after a scheduled follow-up point.

Limitations And Uncertainties

  • Recovery was not given a single standardized definition, and no universally accepted recovery measure was identified.
  • The evidence presented was largely observational, pilot, or implementation-focused; causal effects of screening and follow-up on outcomes were not established.
  • The transcript reports an unpublished global survey suggesting inconsistent implementation of guideline recommendations, but provides no full methods or results.
  • The Lombardia pilot focused on survivors without marked neurological impairment and excluded or did not target some people with severe mental-health problems, limiting generalizability.
  • The feasibility and acceptability of remote follow-up remain unresolved, and the program did not yet answer its main clinical questions.
  • Standard cognitive tests and questionnaires may have limited ecological validity and may not reflect real-world functioning or individual priorities.
  • Repeated assessments can burden survivors, while too few assessments may miss later-emerging needs.
  • The applicability of the described programs to children and adolescents was uncertain; presenters noted that pediatric care would require age-specific approaches and stronger integration with parents and family systems.
  • The transcript ends during the discussion of positive psychological factors, so that part of the research update is incomplete.

Audience Questions

  • How should a hospital with an established registry but no follow-up service begin building a program? Recommendations included gathering clinical experts, assessing organizational readiness, creating a local network step by step, educating clinicians, and providing basic written and online resources.
  • Does the model apply to children and adolescents? The response was that some issues are similar, but age-specific concerns such as return to education and integration with parents and the wider family require adapted approaches.
  • How can survivors be protected from the burden of multiple questionnaires? Presenters discussed developing a survivor-specific outcome measure, avoiding excessive questionnaire time, and combining measures with conversation.
  • When should survivors be assessed? The discussion emphasized that needs may arise at different times, so follow-up should not rely only on a single fixed time point.
  • A survivor questioned absolute measures and argued that progression from discharge through later follow-up may be more meaningful than a single score.

Follow-up reading

  • European guidance on post-cardiac-arrest recovery and follow-up, including the use of measures such as the MoCA, HADS, and EQ-5D-5L, was mentioned but not cited in full.
  • Literature on trauma and PTSD after cardiac arrest, including work referenced in the presentation by Sasha Avalos and by Platt and Fenella, was discussed without complete bibliographic details.
  • A review concerning non-linear trauma-recovery trajectories was recommended by the presenter, but the authors and citation were not fully stated.
  • Research on ecological validity, performance-based functional assessments, informant reports, smartphone-based ecological assessment, existential well-being, and post-traumatic growth may provide relevant background.
  • A developing survivor-specific outcome measure referred to in the transcript as “Cashfall” was mentioned; the exact name and publication details should be verified before searching.

10:01Co-survivors of Cardiac Arrest: Experiences, Evidence, and Support NeedsTime recorded in notes

Overview

The session focused on co-survivors—family members, partners, friends, colleagues, and others affected by someone else’s cardiac arrest. Speakers described lived experiences, research findings, clinical approaches, recruitment challenges, and the need to recognise co-survivors in care and research.

Main Arguments

  • Co-survivors experience their own trauma and recovery needs, even when they were not present at the cardiac arrest.
  • Co-survivors are frequently overlooked because clinical systems focus on the cardiac-arrest patient and may not record relatives or other affected people.
  • Support should begin during hospitalisation and continue after discharge; it should include communication, signposting, screening, peer support, and follow-up.
  • Children and young people who witness or perform CPR should be explicitly included in support and research.
  • Co-survivors may not identify their own need for help or be ready to seek it immediately, making proactive and flexible outreach important.

Key Findings

  • Bernie Glan described persistent hypervigilance, sleep disruption, anxiety about travel and medical emergencies, medication-checking, and distress triggered by reminders several years after her husband’s cardiac arrest.
  • Laurie described co-survivorship as lonely and argued that co-survivors can develop “internalised invisibility” when nobody identifies or validates their support needs.
  • The research presentation reported that up to 42% of relatives may show signs of post-traumatic stress disorder. Reported predictors included female gender, a history of depression, and relatives’ perceptions of information received.
  • Relatives may feel responsible for supporting the patient, need communication with healthcare professionals, and experience an abrupt loss of support at discharge.
  • A 2023 scoping review was said to have identified 44 articles published over approximately 50 years, with few evaluated interventions for improving family outcomes.
  • At a Danish cardiac arrest clinic, co-survivors were integrated into hospital and outpatient care; the clinic had seen more than 1,200 co-survivors and approximately 200 new patients per year, according to the presentation.
  • In the PRS recovery-pathway project, 30 survivors consented and 26 completed the programme, while co-survivor recruitment reached approximately half the target. Those who participated generally attended and valued the six sessions.

Methods Or Evidence

  • Evidence included personal testimonies from co-survivors and survivors, clinical experience from a Danish cardiac arrest centre, a cited scoping review, and research projects funded or supported by the NIHR and other organisations.
  • The PRS project used hospital identification, post-discharge recruitment, and self-referral through a website. Initially, participants had to be within three months of the event; this was extended to 12 months and recruitment was prolonged.
  • A separate qualitative project sought interviews with young people aged 11–18 who had performed CPR on or been involved in the resuscitation of a relative or friend. Recruitment involved schools, clinical research networks, community and sports organisations, support groups, resuscitation organisations, and online routes.
  • The transcript contains some unclear or garbled terms, numerical descriptions, and speaker-name inconsistencies; these details should be verified against the conference materials before formal citation.

Practical Or Clinical Implications

  • Ask co-survivors directly whether they are safe, coping, and in need of support; do not assume that absence of a request means absence of need.
  • Provide clear, repeated oral and written information about what to expect, available support, emotional responses, and follow-up arrangements.
  • Keep co-survivors connected to care after discharge and refer them to psychological services, peer groups, or other support when needed.
  • Consider early screening for distress, anxiety, depression, or post-traumatic stress, while recognising that readiness for support may vary over time.
  • In the immediate response, remain with the co-survivor until another trusted person is present, keep them informed, and provide a contact name or details where appropriate.
  • Include children, teachers, and other witnesses in follow-up planning; explain possible reactions and provide routes to counselling or psychological support.
  • Record and address co-survivor needs despite administrative systems that classify them as relatives rather than patients.

Limitations And Uncertainties

  • The session relied substantially on personal testimony and selected studies; it did not present a systematic synthesis of all available evidence.
  • The prevalence estimate of up to 42% and other clinical figures were stated without enough citation detail in the transcript to assess their methods or generalisability.
  • Co-survivors are difficult to identify and recruit because they may not be recorded in hospital systems, may prioritise the survivor, or may not yet recognise their own need for help.
  • The PRS project had lower-than-target co-survivor recruitment, limiting conclusions about programme effectiveness.
  • The evidence base was described as small, with few tested interventions and limited guidance. The needs of children and young people remain particularly under-researched.

Audience Questions

  • How should first responders support survivors and co-survivors during the first minutes after cardiac arrest? Speakers emphasised staying with the co-survivor, ensuring they are not left alone, maintaining communication, helping connect them with family, and offering a named contact where possible.
  • Does the need for support end after the initial crisis? Speakers stressed that distress can recur years later and that support should remain available rather than stopping once the patient appears medically stable.
  • How can children affected by cardiac arrest be supported? Suggestions included informing schools and teachers, explaining possible reactions, involving relevant medical or ambulance staff where appropriate, and providing referrals to counselling or psychological services.
  • The transcript includes a question about an EMS service, but the wording and answer are unclear and cannot be reliably summarised.

Follow-up reading

  • American Heart Association 2021 guidelines, which the speaker said acknowledge the importance of co-survivors.
  • The 2023 scoping review of co-survivor evidence mentioned during the talk; the specific citation was not provided.
  • Heart Warrior Project and other survivor/co-survivor support networks mentioned by the speakers.
  • The UK Survivorship Network Group mentioned as having been established that year.
  • The PRS recovery-pathway project and the young-people CPR research project described in the session; full study details were not provided.
