08:58Early Cooling During CPR: The PRINCESS II Trial and Targeted Brain CoolingTime recorded in notes

Overview

The speaker discussed initiating cooling during cardiopulmonary resuscitation (CPR), rather than waiting until intensive care admission. The approach uses a portable, non-invasive transnasal cooling device intended to cool the brain early and provide some systemic cooling during transport and subsequent hospital care.

Main arguments

  • Injury from cardiac arrest begins during ischemia and early reperfusion, so treatment should begin as early as possible.
  • Many previous hospital-based cooling trials started treatment several hours after cardiac arrest and used methods that cooled patients slowly.
  • Earlier studies suggested possible neurological benefit in selected patients, particularly those with ventricular fibrillation as the initial rhythm, but broader hospital-based trials did not show clear survival or neurological benefit.
  • The speaker argued that ultra-early cooling may be most promising in patients with shockable rhythms and that this requires evaluation in a trial designed around that population.

Key findings

  • In the speaker's 2019 randomized trial of approximately 700 patients, early cooling showed a 3.1 percentage-point higher favorable outcome proportion than late cooling, but the difference was not statistically significant.
  • The apparent benefit was concentrated among patients whose initial rhythm was ventricular fibrillation, including a stronger post hoc signal for survival with complete neurological recovery (CPC 1).
  • In an initial 100-patient pilot phase of PRINCESS II, the speaker reported good randomization performance and no identified safety issues.
  • More than half of patients had reached target temperature within one hour in the reported temperature data, and target temperature was generally reached within two hours of cardiac arrest.
  • The speaker reported no observed increase in arrhythmias or defibrillation problems to date, and only one patient reportedly had cooling stopped because of hemodynamic issues.

Methods or evidence

  • The cooling system is portable and can be used at the scene, in an ambulance, in the catheterization laboratory, emergency department, and intensive care unit.
  • Nasal catheters deliver a very cold mixture of oxygen or air and liquid coolant. The speaker described cooling during CPR as primarily targeted brain cooling and cooling after return of circulation as also producing systemic cooling.
  • The method does not add cold fluid volume to the circulation, distinguishing it from some earlier prehospital cold-fluid approaches.
  • PRINCESS I compared early prehospital and continued cooling with later intensive-care cooling in a broad cardiac arrest population.
  • PRINCESS II is an ongoing multicenter randomized controlled trial in seven countries, targeting patients with shockable rhythms. It compares early cooling with conventional therapy, described during the discussion as no routine cooling in the control group. The planned sample is approximately 1,000 patients.
  • The speaker stated that PRINCESS II is powered to detect a 9% absolute improvement in the intervention arm, corresponding to a 20% relative improvement, and may be completed in the first quarter of 2029.

Practical or clinical implications

  • If effective, cooling could begin during CPR and continue across prehospital transport and hospital transitions.
  • The approach may avoid some hemodynamic effects associated with infusion of large volumes of cold fluid.
  • Implementation would require airway management before or alongside device placement, ambulance equipment, staff training, transport logistics, and agreement about what constitutes standard care in different countries.
  • The speaker emphasized that current European guidance does not recommend routine cooling after ICU arrival, whereas the proposed strategy tests cooling much earlier in the resuscitation process.

Limitations and uncertainties

  • The favorable subgroup findings from PRINCESS I were post hoc and not statistically definitive; they cannot by themselves change guidelines.
  • PRINCESS II is ongoing, so its effects on survival, complete neurological recovery, and safety are not yet known.
  • The trial's power calculation assumes a relatively large treatment effect, and the speaker acknowledged uncertainty about whether that effect will be observed.
  • Direct non-invasive measurement of brain temperature is not currently available. The speaker cited small studies suggesting that brain temperature may fall faster than esophageal or bladder temperature, but the actual tissue temperature during field treatment cannot be directly confirmed.
  • The potential contribution of cooling in nasal or adjacent anatomical regions to any anti-arrhythmic effect remains uncertain.
  • The logistical feasibility and cost of carrying and operating cooling equipment in ambulances remain unresolved.

Audience questions

  • A question addressed whether intra-arrest cooling might reduce the likelihood of return of spontaneous circulation (ROSC). The speaker said this signal was seen in one analysis but not in a larger population, and that ROSC was actually higher in patients with ventricular fibrillation as the first rhythm in another analysis.
  • The speaker reported no current evidence that the device interferes with defibrillation or increases recurrent ventricular fibrillation, while noting that this is being monitored.
  • Questions concerned arrhythmias, drug variability, electrolyte problems, and other complications associated with hypothermia. The speaker reported no clear excess of these problems so far.
  • The audience asked about ambulance capacity and implementation if both cooling and additional defibrillation equipment were needed. The speaker acknowledged substantial practical challenges but prioritized demonstrating patient benefit first.
  • The audience asked how the device confirms that cortical brain tissue is actually cooled, especially under hot environmental conditions. The speaker stated that direct non-invasive measurement of brain temperature is not currently possible.

Follow-up reading

  • The speaker's 2019 randomized trial of early versus late cooling, published in JAMA; the transcript does not provide the full citation.
  • The PRINCESS II trial protocol and updates at princess2.org.
  • Earlier randomized trials of hospital-based therapeutic hypothermia after cardiac arrest, including the trials referred to in the talk as the LUND and Nicholas trials; full citations were not provided.
  • Current European Resuscitation Council guidance on temperature management after cardiac arrest.

Overview

This interactive workshop examined rehabilitation beginning during intensive care after critical illness, including cardiac arrest. The presenters, occupational and physical therapists from St. Bartholomew’s Hospital in London, discussed physical, cognitive, psychological, and functional consequences of critical illness; rehabilitation across acute, subacute, hospital, and community phases; and post-intensive care syndrome. The workshop included a case study and discussion of barriers to early rehabilitation.

Main arguments

  • Rehabilitation should begin as early as is safe and feasible, rather than waiting until patients are transferred out of intensive care or are fully independent.
  • Rehabilitation should address physical, cognitive, psychological, communicative, and everyday functional abilities together.
  • “Doing something is better than doing nothing,” but the appropriate intensity and dosage remain uncertain and should be tailored to the patient’s daily condition and goals.
  • Rehabilitation is a multidisciplinary responsibility. Nurses, doctors, therapists, families, and community services all contribute to continuity of care.
  • Reducing deep or prolonged sedation, assessing delirium, supporting sleep and orientation, and involving patients and families can facilitate rehabilitation.
  • Care transitions, especially from intensive care to the ward and then to the community, require clearer communication, goal handover, and follow-up.

Key findings

  • ICU-acquired weakness involves critical illness-related myopathy, neuropathy, and immobility-related skeletal-muscle atrophy. The presenters cited muscle loss of up to approximately 30% within 10 days of intensive-care admission and 1–3% per day, while noting that mechanisms and individual trajectories vary.
  • Cognitive dysfunction after critical illness commonly involves processing speed, attention, memory, and executive functions. The presenters cited substantial prevalence at three months, one year, and two years, with risk associated with delirium, deep or prolonged sedation, hypoxemia, inflammation, and glucose dysregulation.
  • Delirium was described as a major modifiable concern, with longer delirium associated with greater risk of persistent cognitive impairment.
  • High-intensity rehabilitation did not improve outcomes compared with regular rehabilitation in the cited trial, and standing ventilated patients may impose a high oxygen cost. The presenters therefore cautioned against assuming that more intense exercise is always better.
  • At St. Bartholomew’s, common barriers included sedation, agitation, cardiovascular support or instability, staffing, equipment, and cultural assumptions about who should provide rehabilitation.
  • Quality-improvement work described by the presenters included delirium-management changes, broader team education, delirium ward rounds, and revised criteria for mobilising selected postoperative patients receiving noradrenaline.
  • The case study illustrated early neurological and functional assessment, communication support, personal-care practice, cognitive assessment, showering as a functional goal when safe, and structured handover at transfer.

Methods or evidence

  • The workshop drew on UK guidance and reports, including GPICS, NICE guideline CG83, and the National Confidential Inquiry into Patient Outcome and Death report “Recovery Beyond Survival.”
  • The presenters discussed evidence and clinical experience concerning ICU-acquired weakness, sedation interruption, early mobilisation, rehabilitation on ECMO, delirium, cognitive impairment, and post-intensive care syndrome.
  • Measures mentioned included the Medical Research Council sum score, CPAX, modified Rankin Scale, Functional Independence Measure/Functional Assessment Measure, Barthel Index, Intensive Care Mobility Scale, CAM-ICU, Coma Recovery Scale-Revised, Wessex Head Injury Matrix, ACE-3, MoCA, RUDAS, and Symbol Digit Modalities Test.
  • A case study involved a 55-year-old man after out-of-hospital ventricular-fibrillation cardiac arrest, with bystander CPR, four shocks, approximately 21 minutes of downtime, mechanical ventilation, sedation, severe weakness, and hypoxic brain injury on MRI.
  • Local quality-improvement work used barrier identification, data collection, multidisciplinary education, and pathway changes. Specific local results included a reported increase in eligible mobilisation among selected patients receiving noradrenaline after a change in practice.

Practical or clinical implications

  • Assess patients as soon as sedation is appropriately reduced or stopped and medical stability permits; do not automatically wait for extubation or tracheostomy.
  • Begin with achievable activities such as turning, supported sitting, oral care, grooming, communication, orientation, and functional self-care.
  • Tailor rehabilitation to the patient’s current tolerance, avoiding rigid assumptions about timing, repetitions, or discharge milestones.
  • Coordinate therapy with nursing, medical care, nutrition, airway management, sedation plans, and periods of cardiovascular or respiratory stability.
  • Use daily sedation interruption when clinically appropriate, minimise deep or prolonged sedation, and assess and manage delirium systematically.
  • Support normal sleep–wake patterns, natural light, orientation boards, communication access, family involvement, and sensory stimulation.
  • Set meaningful goals based on the patient’s identity and priorities, such as returning to valued leisure, family, work, or self-care activities.
  • Plan explicitly for the transition from intensive care to the ward and community, including rehabilitation goals, cognitive and psychological needs, family support, equipment, and follow-up.
  • Consider business cases, local data, and quality-improvement methods when seeking staffing, equipment, or dedicated occupational-therapy and rehabilitation resources.

Limitations and uncertainties

  • The transcript does not identify the conference, talk title, speaker names in the metadata fields, publication details, or complete citations for the studies mentioned.
  • The optimal dose, frequency, intensity, and timing of ICU rehabilitation remain uncertain; the presenters emphasised individual tailoring.
  • Rehabilitation studies and outcome measures may not capture subtle or short-term functional, cognitive, or neurological improvements equally well.
  • Some measures discussed were considered time-consuming, insufficiently sensitive in the acute setting, or difficult to implement consistently.
  • The evidence and examples largely reflected UK practice at a large specialist cardiac ICU and may not transfer directly to settings with different staffing, equipment, resources, or professional roles.
  • Recovery trajectories are highly variable, particularly when ICU-acquired weakness coexists with hypoxic-ischaemic brain injury, so precise prognostic timelines are unreliable.
  • The transcript reports selected local quality-improvement findings but does not provide full study designs, comparison groups, statistical analyses, or long-term outcomes.

Audience questions

  • What is the appropriate definition and timing of “early mobilisation,” particularly for patients after cardiac arrest or on ECMO?
  • How often and how intensely should rehabilitation be delivered to prevent or reduce ICU-acquired weakness?
  • How can cognition, orientation, communication, and delirium prevention be supported during intensive care?
  • What can nurses and other staff do when an ICU has no occupational therapist?
  • How can staffing, equipment, sedation practices, and unit culture be addressed as barriers to mobilisation?
  • Should standardised cognitive assessments be used in intensive care, or are functional and informal assessments more appropriate until the patient reaches the ward?
  • How can rehabilitation intensity be maintained after transfer from intensive care, when ward staffing and resources are lower?
  • What support should be provided to families, including socioeconomic, psychological, and practical support?

Follow-up reading

  • Intensive Care Medicine Society and Faculty of Intensive Care Medicine: Guidelines for the Provision of Intensive Care Services (GPICS), including Version 3 referenced in the workshop.
  • NICE guideline CG83: Rehabilitation after critical illness.
  • National Confidential Inquiry into Patient Outcome and Death: “Recovery Beyond Survival.”
  • Literature on ICU-acquired weakness, delirium, sedation interruption, early mobilisation, rehabilitation during ECMO, and post-intensive care syndrome.
  • Further reading specifically mentioned by the presenters included work on the oxygen cost of rehabilitation and the distinction between exercise and rehabilitation, as well as feasibility studies of early mobilisation. Full bibliographic details were not provided in the transcript.

08:36The Brain After Cardiac ArrestTime recorded in notes

Overview

The talk provided a high-level overview of brain injury after cardiac arrest, covering epidemiology, ischemia and reperfusion injury, limits of brain healing, secondary brain injury, long-term outcomes, and recovery. The central message was that current care is inadequate across these areas and that injury mechanisms are complex, heterogeneous, time-dependent, and difficult to measure at the bedside.

Main arguments

  • The brain remains vulnerable throughout the cardiac-arrest course: during ischemia, reperfusion, and the subsequent intensive-care period.
  • Brain injury after cardiac arrest is not a single process. Multiple mechanisms vary between patients and over time, making single-target therapies unlikely to be sufficient.
  • The brain has limited capacity to restore its original networks because recovery requires more than replacing cells; it requires re-establishing complex synaptic connections and long axonal pathways.
  • Current treatment is largely high-quality general critical care rather than therapy specifically targeted to the mechanisms or timing of post-arrest brain injury.
  • Recovery should be assessed beyond hospital survival, including cognitive, psychological, physical, functional, and participatory outcomes.

Key findings

  • In an illustrative cohort of 100 patients resuscitated from cardiac arrest who survive to hospital admission, the speaker estimated that approximately 30 recover well long-term, 10 survive with persistent neurological, functional, or psychological deficits, 40 die from consequences of brain injury, and 20 die primarily from non-neurological causes; the cohort was described as highly mixed.
  • Even patients who are initially awake or responsive to verbal commands can experience clinically significant neurological problems, with incidence described as upwards of 40% in those groups.
  • During the first year after hospital discharge, cognitive impairment is common, particularly difficulties with memory, attention, and executive function. Psychological symptoms, fatigue, and physical or functional limitations are also frequent.
  • Beyond one year, the prevalence of many problems decreases but does not reach zero; several remain at frequencies not very different from those observed during the first year.
  • Evidence for post-acute interventions is limited. The nurse-driven Alaska trial showed modest but significant benefits in emotional well-being, return to work, and identification of problems for targeted resolution in just under 200 survivors.
  • Overall, the speaker characterized the evidence base as covering only a small fraction of the broad range of recovery needs.

Methods or evidence

  • The presentation synthesized older and contemporary literature, including historical monographs and laboratory research.
  • Mechanistic explanations drew substantially on laboratory studies involving cells and animal models, while acknowledging that bedside physiology is more complex and heterogeneous.
  • The ischemic cascade described included ATP depletion, failure of sodium-potassium pumps, membrane depolarization, glutamate accumulation, cellular swelling, calcium-related injury, and toxic metabolic accumulation.
  • Reperfusion was described as amplifying injury through reactive oxygen species, including effects related to accumulated succinate; laboratory studies were cited as showing that this process occurs within approximately 10–15 minutes after return of spontaneous circulation.
  • Secondary injury mechanisms discussed included impaired perfusion, cerebrovascular resistance abnormalities, seizures or EEG hyperexcitability, blood-brain barrier disruption, edema, inflammation, and multiple forms of cell death.
  • Clinical evidence mentioned included the Alaska trial, ICU-diary research from other critical-illness populations, structured exercise and rehabilitation, post-acute ICU recovery clinics, and small case studies or case series. Many interventions were described as general rather than cardiac-arrest-specific.

Practical or clinical implications

  • Early timing may be important because several injury cascades are established or amplified soon after arrest and reperfusion; interventions applied hours to days later may miss key biological windows.
  • Future research may need earlier phenotyping, risk stratification, prognostication, and personalized treatment matched to the right patient and the right time.
  • Post-discharge care should address cognitive, psychological, physical, functional, and participatory problems rather than focusing only on survival to hospital discharge.
  • General healthy-lifestyle measures, including physical activity, adequate sleep, and a balanced diet, were presented as reasonable, but no unique or targeted post-arrest benefit was established for these measures.
  • The speaker stated that evidence does not currently show that supplements such as fish oil or vitamin C specifically improve post-arrest brain recovery.
  • Clinicians should account for non-neurological illness, comorbidity, treatment decisions, and withdrawal of life-sustaining treatment when interpreting outcomes and evaluating interventions.

Limitations and uncertainties

  • The epidemiological figures were approximate and applied to a mixed cohort with varied levels of initial responsiveness and neurological injury.
  • Much mechanistic evidence comes from laboratory models and may not directly represent individual patients in the intensive care unit.
  • The causes of persistent fatigue after cardiac arrest remain unclear; some fatigue may reflect general critical illness rather than cardiac-arrest-specific brain biology.
  • The extent and clinical significance of neurogenesis and neural plasticity after injury remain incompletely understood.
  • Many trials have been neutral, and existing interventions are often general critical-care or rehabilitation approaches rather than targeted therapies.
  • Mortality and functional outcomes are confounded by non-neurological organ failure, comorbidities, re-arrest, treatment limitations, and patient choices regarding continued intensive care.
  • The talk did not establish effective dietary, supplement-based, or other specific lifestyle treatments for enhancing brain recovery.

Audience questions

  • A survivor asked whether lifestyle or dietary measures after discharge can help the brain recover. The speaker said evidence for a specific targeted intervention is limited; general healthy-lifestyle practices are probably beneficial, but no unique post-arrest benefit is established.
  • The same audience member asked why fatigue is so common. The speaker said the reason is unknown, noted that fatigue also occurs after other critical illnesses and injuries, and suggested that it may sometimes improve more than other symptoms during the first year.
  • An audience member asked how deaths from non-neurological causes affect prognostication and interpretation of studies. The speaker emphasized that outcomes are complicated by comorbidity, non-brain organ failure, re-arrest, treatment decisions, and withdrawal of life-sustaining treatment, and that studies need to account for these factors to detect biological treatment effects.

Follow-up reading

  • Specific citations and a formal reading list were not provided in the transcript.
  • Topics identified for further reading include the original cardiac-arrest research literature, ischemia and reperfusion injury, post-cardiac-arrest secondary brain injury, the Alaska post-acute recovery trial, ICU diaries, structured rehabilitation, and post-acute ICU recovery clinics.
08:56Delirium management in the ICUTime recorded in notes

Overview

The talk focused on delirium in general ICU populations, including cardiac patients. Delirium was described as acute encephalopathy and clinically important organ dysfunction that is common, fluctuating, difficult to detect, and associated with adverse outcomes for patients and families. The central message was to protect the brain, support the person, and promote recovery beyond the ICU.

Main arguments

  • Delirium is not simply confusion or agitation; it represents brain dysfunction during critical illness.
  • Hypoactive delirium is common and easily missed because patients may appear quiet or cooperative.
  • Screening should be repeated throughout the day and combined with clinical assessment of causes and risk factors.
  • Prevention and management should emphasize multicomponent supportive care, including mobilization, sleep, family involvement, pain and sedation assessment, and treatment of reversible contributors.
  • Medication is not a substitute for identifying causes and providing supportive care.
  • Recovery and cognitive effects after ICU discharge should be considered, although the best follow-up model remains uncertain.

Key findings

  • Delirium was reported to affect approximately 25–35% of the general ICU population.
  • Hypoactive delirium was described as the most common motor subtype, accounting for about half of delirium cases in the cited review.
  • In the cited systematic review, hypoactive delirium was associated with longer ICU and hospital stays and higher mortality than hyperactive or mixed delirium.
  • Delirium severity tools such as CAM-ICU-7 and the severity version of the Intensive Care Delirium Screening Checklist can provide graded scores rather than only yes/no classifications.
  • Greater implementation of the ABCDEF bundle, extended with sleep-related care as “G,” was associated in a cited California study with better outcomes; the speaker estimated a possible 25–50% reduction in delirium- and coma-days.
  • The cited 2022 AICU study of approximately 1,000 adult ICU patients with delirium found no statistically significant difference in its primary outcome, days alive and out of hospital at 90 days, between treatment groups. A statistically significant mortality difference favored haloperidol, but the reason for this finding was not known.
  • The speaker stated that current evidence is insufficient to recommend for or against routine antipsychotic treatment over usual care for ICU delirium.

Methods or evidence

  • The prevalence and outcomes of delirium subtypes were discussed using findings from a systematic review.
  • Screening approaches discussed included the RASS for assessing arousal and ability to participate, CAM-ICU-7, and a severity index/checklist based on the ICDSC.
  • Risk factors were divided into non-modifiable vulnerabilities, such as older age, cognitive impairment, comorbidity, and previous delirium, and potentially modifiable precipitants, such as hypoxia, circulatory problems, medication withdrawal, immobility, pain, sedation, sleep disruption, and psychological disturbances.
  • Evidence for multicomponent care was linked to the ABCDEF bundle and its extension to sleep-related care.
  • The speaker described a randomized study of approximately 1,000 adult ICU patients with delirium, excluding patients with traumatic brain injury and pre-ICU cognitive impairment. The intervention was reported as haloperidol 2.5 mg three times daily, with additional doses up to 20 mg per 24 hours, and an escape protocol involving propofol, dexmedetomidine, or midazolam.
  • A single-site Australian study was mentioned as finding less time in hyperactive delirium with dexmedetomidine than haloperidol, but it was not presented as evidence resolving broader questions about sleep management.
  • A patient account, referred to as Oskar, illustrated that ICU delusions and hallucinations may remain important experiences after discharge and should be listened to without dismissing the patient’s experience.

Practical or clinical implications

  • Do not assume that a quiet or easy-to-care-for patient is free of delirium.
  • Screen repeatedly across the full 24-hour ICU stay because delirium fluctuates.
  • Use severity scores to monitor changes over time rather than relying only on delirium present/absent classifications.
  • Treat a positive screen as a prompt to investigate current contributors, including pain, medications, sedation, hypoxia, circulation, withdrawal, immobility, sleep, and psychological distress.
  • Promote mobilization, rehabilitation, planned activity, sleep-supportive care, and family involvement.
  • Use antipsychotics cautiously and do not treat them as a replacement for supportive care or evaluation of underlying causes.
  • Ask patients after ICU discharge about perceived delusions or hallucinations, listen without simply telling them that the experiences were untrue, and consider their possible effect on rehabilitation and recovery.

Limitations and uncertainties

  • The reported prevalence and evidence primarily concern general ICU populations and include cardiac patients; applicability to other populations was not established.
  • Screening identifies possible delirium but does not explain its cause.
  • The optimal delirium follow-up model after ICU discharge remains uncertain.
  • Evidence for routine antipsychotic treatment is described as low-certainty and insufficient to support a clear recommendation.
  • The mortality signal favoring haloperidol in the cited study was not explained, and the speaker suggested that causes of death should be adjudicated.
  • It remains unclear whether haloperidol is superior to quetiapine or other antipsychotics.
  • The effectiveness of dexmedetomidine, propofol, or midazolam for sleep and delirium prevention is unresolved, and potential next-day sedation or “hangover” effects were noted.
  • The evidence does not establish a drug treatment that restores cognition in hypoactive delirium.

Audience questions

  • Is there a drug that improves cognition in hypoactive delirium? The speaker stated that there is no clear answer and emphasized supportive care and attention to causes.
  • Should dexmedetomidine or propofol be started at night to promote sleep, and do these drugs prevent delirium? The speaker said that the evidence is insufficient and that sleep management in critically ill patients remains an open area.
  • Why was mortality lower with haloperidol in the cited study? The speaker said this is unknown and suggested examining adjudicated causes of death.
  • Is haloperidol better than quetiapine or other antipsychotics? The speaker reported that recent systematic reviews have not demonstrated a difference between antipsychotic groups.

Follow-up reading

  • The ABCDEF bundle and its extension to sleep-related care, as discussed in the talk.
  • CAM-ICU-7 and the severity version of the Intensive Care Delirium Screening Checklist.
  • The 2018 PADIS guidance and the update referred to by the speaker regarding mobilization, rehabilitation, and antipsychotic treatment.
  • The cited 2022 AICU randomized study of haloperidol for ICU delirium.
  • Systematic reviews on hypoactive delirium, delirium severity scoring, and comparisons among antipsychotic agents.
10:22Conference talk on neuropsychological outcomes after cardiac arrestTime recorded in notes

Overview

The speaker, Pauline, discussed cognitive impairment after cardiac arrest, focusing on assessment methods, discrepancies between test performance and reported complaints, challenges in defining impairment, and implications for clinical practice and research. She drew on PhD research conducted from 2020 to 2025.

Main arguments

  • Cognitive impairment is common after cardiac arrest, particularly affecting memory, attention, and executive functioning.
  • No single assessment method captures all relevant aspects of post-cardiac-arrest cognitive functioning.
  • Standardized neuropsychological tests are valuable but may not reflect patients’ everyday experiences.
  • The field would benefit from a consensus definition of cognitive impairment, including a standardized test battery, normative data, and agreed cutoffs.
  • Cognitive functioning should often be treated as a spectrum or continuous variable rather than only as a dichotomous impairment/no-impairment outcome.

Key findings

  • Approximately half of cardiac-arrest survivors have cognitive impairment, according to the presentation.
  • In the cited MoCA validity study, a cutoff score of 26 produced 86% sensitivity and 70% specificity in the cardiac-arrest population.
  • The speaker recommended full neuropsychological assessment for people scoring below 26 on the MoCA.
  • Prior studies cited in the talk found that patients sometimes performed poorly on cognitive tests while reporting relatively few cognitive complaints.
  • The speaker’s study found no evidence that this discrepancy was explained by reduced self-awareness when comparing patient and proxy reports, although a recent study cited in the talk found some evidence of reduced self-awareness among patients with the lowest cognitive-test scores.
  • Response shift, including increased gratitude after survival, was proposed as a possible explanation for low self-reported complaints, but this remains an untested hypothesis.

Methods or evidence

  • Full neuropsychological assessment was described as involving multiple tests of domains such as memory, attention, and processing speed. Scores are compared with normative data matched for age, gender, and education; scores approximately 1.5 to 2 standard deviations below the mean may be classified as abnormal.
  • The speaker discussed a study evaluating the validity of the Montreal Cognitive Assessment in cardiac-arrest survivors.
  • A study examined possible lack of self-awareness by comparing self-reports with proxy reports.
  • Alternative approaches included performance-based assessments such as the Multiple Errands Test, ecological momentary assessment using repeated smartphone-based measurements, and single-case experimental designs for evaluating intervention effects over time.
  • The speaker noted substantial heterogeneity across studies in the number of tests used and cutoff criteria for defining cognitive impairment.

Practical or clinical implications

  • Screen for cognitive impairment approximately three months after cardiac arrest, preferably using the MoCA, because most spontaneous cognitive recovery was described as occurring during the first three months.
  • Consider full neuropsychological assessment when screening indicates possible impairment and resources permit.
  • Combine standardized cognitive tests with patient complaints, proxy information, and performance-based assessments.
  • Provide patients with feedback about their performance and practical coping information when possible.
  • Computerized assessments may improve efficiency, but in-person assessment can provide important observation, feedback, and understanding of the patient’s experience.
  • Researchers should consider analyzing cognitive functioning as a continuous variable when appropriate.

Limitations and uncertainties

  • A full neuropsychological assessment can take three to four hours, is demanding for patients, and may not be feasible for everyone.
  • Standardized tests have limited ecological validity and may not correspond closely to everyday functioning.
  • The MoCA sensitivity and specificity values were reported for the cited study and may not generalize to every setting or population.
  • The explanation involving response shift was presented as a hypothesis without supporting data.
  • There is no agreed-upon definition of cognitive impairment in the cardiac-arrest or broader acquired-brain-injury field, making studies difficult to compare or combine.
  • The speaker’s views on computerized assessments were based partly on studies she had not conducted herself and were presented with some uncertainty.

Audience questions

  • On computerized cognitive assessments: Pauline viewed them as practical and potentially effective, especially given the time demands of traditional assessments, but emphasized the benefits of seeing patients in person and hearing about their experiences.
  • On developing a core outcome set: Audience members recommended involving patients and families so that selected measures are meaningful to them.
  • On scope of a consensus definition: An audience member suggested developing a framework for acquired brain injury generally rather than cardiac arrest alone; Pauline agreed that this could improve generalizability.
  • On clinical neuropsychological testing: An audience member described full testing two to three months after cardiac arrest as valuable because patients receive feedback and coping advice. Pauline agreed that feedback is useful in both clinical practice and research.

Follow-up reading

  • Steinbush and colleagues: study cited regarding low cognitive-test performance and relatively few cognitive complaints.
  • Jensen and colleagues: study cited as replicating the discrepancy between cognitive-test results and self-reported complaints.
  • Van Der Waal: recent study cited as finding some evidence of reduced self-awareness among patients with the lowest cognitive-test scores.
  • Literature on the Multiple Errands Test, ecological momentary assessment, and single-case experimental designs may provide further background on alternative assessment approaches.
  • Consensus work on mild cognitive impairment in dementia was mentioned as a possible model for developing guidance in cardiac arrest or acquired brain injury.

Overview

Christine, a researcher and clinical psychologist at the University of Washington, discussed psychological distress after cardiac arrest and the potential role of tailored, exposure-based cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT). The talk focused particularly on “cardiac anxiety”: fear of recurrent cardiac events, hypervigilance to bodily sensations, and avoidance of activities or situations perceived as threatening.

Main arguments

  • Psychological distress after cardiac arrest is common, can persist beyond acute recovery, and affects quality of life, functioning, health behaviors, physical health, and healthcare use.
  • General psychological treatments and measures may not adequately address the specific experience of fearing internal cardiac sensations and recurrence.
  • Cardiac anxiety differs from, but can co-occur with, post-traumatic stress disorder. Cardiac anxiety is primarily future- and body-focused, whereas PTSD is more closely tied to the traumatic event and its memories or reminders.
  • Exposure-based CBT may interrupt the cycle of fear, hypervigilance, and avoidance by helping patients safely re-engage with feared sensations and activities.
  • Psychological care should be integrated with medical care and tailored to the patient population, clinical status, recovery goals, and cognitive or family needs.

Key findings

  • The speaker reported approximate rates after cardiac arrest of one in four patients experiencing anxiety and one in five experiencing depression or PTSD symptoms.
  • In prior work with atrial fibrillation patients, tailored CBT improved disease-specific quality of life and reduced healthcare consumption, without changing measured atrial fibrillation burden.
  • In patients after myocardial infarction, digital CBT improved cardiac anxiety and disease-specific health status, including physical limitations and quality of life.
  • A feasibility pilot and subsequent randomized controlled trials in cardiac surgery populations were described as showing encouraging results; larger implementation studies are underway.
  • The cardiac-arrest application remains understudied and was presented as a research possibility rather than an established treatment.

Methods or evidence

  • The intervention program was developed through approximately 10 years of collaboration between psychology and cardiology at Karolinska University Hospital in Stockholm, with multidisciplinary and patient-group input.
  • The approach uses CBT, behavioral analysis, patient-specific recovery goals, systematic exposure, patient education, and relapse prevention.
  • Interventions were described as typically eight-week, digital, text-based programs with individual psychologist support and medical-team consultation.
  • Examples of exposure activities included running in place, hyperventilating, and, for cardiac surgery patients, lifting objects or using the chest again.
  • Prior studies progressed from pilot testing to efficacy trials and, for atrial fibrillation and acute coronary syndrome, effectiveness and implementation trials.
  • Participants were medically assessed, had guideline-directed medical therapy, lacked contraindications to physical activity, and were screened for other severe psychiatric conditions requiring different treatment. In the study discussed during the audience question, patients had completed cardiac rehabilitation before enrollment and were approximately six months beyond the cardiac event.

Practical or clinical implications

  • Clinicians can ask whether worry about the heart prevents patients from doing activities they want to do, alongside use of the validated Cardiac Anxiety Questionnaire.
  • Assessment should distinguish cardiac anxiety from PTSD while recognizing that both may be present and require different treatment targets.
  • Exposure-based treatment should be delivered only after appropriate medical assessment and in coordination with the cardiac care team.

Overview

The talk presented cognitive rehabilitation as an individualized, holistic process aimed primarily at improving everyday functioning after brain injury, including cardiac arrest and anoxic brain injury.   • Behavioral changes result from interactions among neurological dysfunction, environmental demands, and pre-existing behavioral patterns; brain injury alone does not determine functioning.

  • Rehabilitation should build on preserved strengths, improve awareness, support psychological well-being, and involve family and other members of the patient’s network.
  • Metacognitive strategy training is central for problems involving awareness, attention, memory, and executive functioning.
  • Rehabilitation progresses through assessment, acquisition, application, generalization, and outcome measurement.

Key findings

  • Spontaneous recovery is fastest early after injury and gradually decreases, but the speaker stated that rehabilitation is never necessarily too late because the brain remains plastic.
  • Restorative training may be appropriate for selected attention or working-memory problems, whereas memory impairments generally require compensatory strategies.
  • External aids such as smartphones, alarms, schedules, routines, and environmental modifications can support everyday functioning.
  • Computer-based training should be monitored by a therapist and followed up continuously; it should not be treated as a stand-alone intervention.
  • Attention should be assessed by type, including sustained, selective, shifting, divided, and goal-directed attention.
  • Severe injuries may limit generalization from clinic exercises to daily life, making home- or context-based training important.
  • Improvements in awareness can initially make patients feel worse because they recognize their deficits; objective measurement can demonstrate actual progress.

Methods or evidence

  • The presentation was described as a brief summary based on a cognitive rehabilitation manual and textbook, which the speaker recommended.
  • The speaker referred to evidence favoring management and compensation, metacognitive strategy training, attention process training, time-pressure management, goal-management training, self-talk, and “stop-think-act” strategies for selected difficulties.

  • A cardiac-arrest survivor returning to university work illustrated goal setting, smartphone scheduling and alarms, attention training, and measurable short-term objectives.

  • Begin with assessment of cognitive impairments, awareness, emotional functioning, everyday problem situations, strengths, goals, and social supports.
  • Identify which executive functions are impaired rather than treating executive functioning as a single ability.
  • Use situation-specific feedback and metacognitive strategy training when deficits reduce awareness; provide this across relevant rehabilitation activities, not only in psychology sessions.
  • For psychological resistance or crisis reactions, avoid direct confrontation, build trust, increase perceived control, offer choices, and provide psychological support when needed.
  • Educate patients, families, and the wider network about the injury and cognitive consequences.
  • Explain what is being trained and why, then progress toward independent and increasingly automatic use of strategies.
  • Set functional, measurable goals linked to home, work, and social life, and regularly review outcomes to determine whether the intervention or training level should change.
