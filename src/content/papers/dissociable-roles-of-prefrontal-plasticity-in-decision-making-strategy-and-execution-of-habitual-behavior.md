## Summary

Habits are usually defined by a shift in how actions are chosen: behaviour that
was initially sensitive to current goals and reward value becomes more
automatic. This study argues that this alone is incomplete. The authors
separately measured the decision strategy used by a mouse and the amount of
habitual behaviour it performed, such as lever-pressing frequency and duration.

Male mice first learnt a sucrose lever-press task using a variable-ratio
schedule, which produced goal-directed responding. The schedule was then
switched to a variable-interval structure, allowing the same animal to develop
a habitual strategy within a defined period. Outcome-devaluation and omission
tests confirmed the strategy shift. Yet individual differences in the amount of
lever pressing did not correlate with the strength of habit as indexed by
devaluation sensitivity, indicating that execution level and decision strategy
are distinct dimensions.

The authors then linked these two dimensions to different plastic changes in
prefrontal cortex. Goal-directed control was associated with potentiation in
layer 5 of anterior cingulate cortex (ACC), and this potentiation diminished
after habit formation. Manipulating ACC activity could push animals toward a
habitual strategy or restore goal-directed responding without changing how much
they executed the action.

In contrast, the amount of habitual responding correlated with potentiation in
layer 5 of lateral orbitofrontal cortex (LOFC). Erasing LOFC plasticity reduced
the execution level without changing the mice's habitual decision strategy.
Conversely, erasing ACC plasticity changed strategy without altering execution.
Together, the findings support a dual-regulation model of habit.

## Why This Matters

A person can act habitually yet execute that action at very different levels:
the decision may be automatic, while the frequency or persistence of the action
is separately controlled. That distinction is especially relevant for thinking
about maladaptive habits, where excessive execution—not merely automaticity—is
often what makes the behaviour clinically impairing.

By mapping the two components onto separable prefrontal plasticity, the paper
offers a framework for more targeted biological models of compulsivity and
addiction. It may help explain why interventions that influence the
goal-directed versus habitual balance do not necessarily reduce the amount of a
habitual behaviour.

## Caveats and Limitations

The behavioural model is an experimentally constrained sucrose-reward task in
male mice. It cannot directly model the motivational, emotional, social and
long-term features of human compulsions or addictions.

Prefrontal regions do not operate in isolation; striatal, dopaminergic and
other circuits are well known to contribute to habits. This study identifies
dissociable prefrontal mechanisms within its task, rather than replacing broader
models of habit circuitry.

## Key Takeaways

- Separates habitual decision strategy from the quantity of habitual action
  execution.
- A sequential ratio-to-interval task tracks both processes within the same
  mouse.
- ACC plasticity supports goal-directed over habitual decision-making.
- LOFC plasticity predicts and regulates how much habitual behaviour is
  executed.
- Targeted removal of each plasticity changes strategy or execution
  independently.
- Provides a dual-regulatory circuit model relevant to compulsive and addictive
  behaviour.
