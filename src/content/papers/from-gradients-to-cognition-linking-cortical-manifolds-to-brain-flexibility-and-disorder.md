## Summary

This mini-review brings together research on **cortical gradients**: continuous patterns of variation across the cortex in connectivity, microstructure and function. Researchers derive gradients by reducing complex brain measurements to a small number of dimensions. The resulting **manifold** places regions with more similar measured properties closer together, providing a way to describe large-scale relationships between brain systems.

A widely studied principal functional gradient extends from unimodal sensory and motor regions towards transmodal association cortex, including the default mode network. The authors connect this organization to genetic, developmental, evolutionary and microstructural evidence. They discuss findings that functional and microstructural gradients correspond more closely in primary regions and diverge more in transmodal cortex, potentially supporting the flexible integration of information across networks.

The review also describes how these representations change with experience. Studies of learning report changes in regions' positions and periods of manifold expansion and contraction as task demands evolve. Reward learning and cerebellar–cortical interactions are discussed as additional influences. These observations offer a way to study changes in distributed brain organization, although the meaning of any particular geometric change depends on how the manifold was constructed.

In clinical contexts, the authors review altered gradient organization in autism, depression and schizophrenia, as well as reorganization after stroke. They describe a macaque stroke study in which changes involving default mode and limbic regions were associated with behavioural recovery. These findings motivate research into network-level plasticity and possible biomarkers; their clinical usefulness for individual patients remains to be established.

## Why This Matters

Gradients offer a complementary way to think about cognition as emerging from relationships among distributed systems. This is relevant to understanding cognitive flexibility, learning and recovery after injury, where changes may extend beyond a single region or conventional cognitive domain.

## Caveats and Limitations

This is a synthesis of existing studies, not a new study testing a single causal model. Gradient maps can differ with data modality, preprocessing, parcellation, similarity measures and embedding method; maps derived from different inputs should not automatically be treated as equivalent. Motion and physiological noise, limited test–retest reliability and differences between scanners or sites can affect results. The cited associations do not establish that a gradient alteration causes a symptom or recovery, and individual-level diagnostic or prognostic performance is not yet established. The authors also discuss critiques that continuous maps are statistical summaries and may obscure evidence for discrete cortical areas; both perspectives can be informative.

## Key Takeaways

- Cortical gradients summarize gradual differences in measured brain organization across regions.
- A prominent functional gradient spans sensory and motor areas through to transmodal association cortex.
- The review links gradient organization to development and microstructure, and describes changes during learning and after stroke.
- Clinical associations are promising research directions, but gradient measures are not established individual biomarkers.
- How a gradient is derived matters for its interpretation; continuous and area-based accounts are complementary.
