## Summary

This study examines how neural representations of experiences change as the
experiences become established memories. The researchers used wireless,
high-density recordings from hippocampal and associated regions in two freely
moving macaques. The animals performed item-context sequences that were either
newly learned on that day, recalled one day later or familiar from more than two
weeks earlier (on average, around six weeks), and were then recorded during
sleep.

The authors identified co-active neuronal ensembles, termed cell assemblies,
that were selectively associated with the different sequence conditions. All
categories of assembly reactivated during sleep, especially around hippocampal
sharp-wave ripples. However, assemblies biased toward older and recent memories
showed stronger task activation and stronger, more frequent ripple-related sleep
reactivation than assemblies biased toward newly learned sequences. Older
assemblies also showed less gradual change, or drift, across task and sleep.

The key extension is the analysis of how assemblies interact with one another.
Pairs of old and recent assemblies showed more stable coupling from behaviour to
sleep and longer-lived co-activation than new assemblies. The authors call
these persistent higher-order links metassemblies. Their network analyses further
suggest that the neuronal members of older assemblies are more locally
interconnected. Within CA1, superficial and deep pyramidal cells were more
likely to contribute to integrated assemblies as the associated memory aged.

## Why This Matters

Memory consolidation is often described as replay that strengthens a newly
formed trace. This paper suggests a broader process in which experiences become
embedded in a changing organisation of neural ensembles. Older memories were not
merely retained: their assemblies were more stable and better connected, whereas
newer assemblies appeared to be still reorganising.

The primate recordings are particularly valuable because most mechanistic work
on cell assemblies and sleep reactivation has been conducted in rodents. The
findings provide a bridge toward understanding how long-lived, linked memories
might be represented in brains with a richer behavioural repertoire.

## Caveats and Limitations

Only two macaques were studied, with neural coverage determined by the recording
design and a task involving repeated, well-defined item sequences. The results
therefore should not be assumed to characterise all forms of declarative,
episodic or autobiographical memory, nor to translate directly to humans.

The study is observational at the neural-mechanism level. Stronger sleep
reactivation and more stable network structure covary with memory age, but the
experiments do not manipulate replay, sleep or network coupling to show that any
one of them causes long-term retention.

## Key Takeaways

- Tracks content-specific neuronal assemblies from task performance into sleep
  in freely moving macaques.
- Older and recent memory assemblies reactivate more strongly during
  sharp-wave-ripple-associated sleep activity than new assemblies.
- Older assemblies show less drift and greater local network interconnectedness.
- Recent and old assemblies form persistent task-to-sleep coupling patterns
  termed metassemblies.
- CA1 pyramidal-cell participation in integrated assemblies increases with
  memory age.
- Offers primate evidence that sleep-related consolidation involves progressive
  reorganisation as well as preservation of memory traces.
