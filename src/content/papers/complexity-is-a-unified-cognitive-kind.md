## Summary

This paper tests whether people represent complexity as a common, domain-general property rather than as a collection of unrelated judgments about particular kinds of stimuli. Across 11 experiments involving 1,500 participants, the authors compared complexity learning and judgment across visual shapes, dot arrays, melodies, letter strings, mathematical expressions and tactile forms. In reward-transfer tasks, outcomes learned for complex versus simple stimuli generalized to a different stimulus domain, indicating that participants treated complexity as a property that could carry over between formats. Follow-up experiments found that this transfer was automatic enough to influence task-irrelevant judgments, and that people showed correlated preferences for simplicity across domains: for example, people who preferred simple shapes also tended to prefer simple melodies. The findings support a type-independent representation of information density, compatible with the idea that the mind uses a shared representational currency for complexity across qualitatively different inputs.

## Why This Matters

Complexity influences attention, learning, memory and aesthetic preference, but has usually been studied separately within each domain. This paper offers evidence that the mind can abstract a common notion of complexity across vision, audition, language, mathematics and touch.

## Caveats and Limitations

The experiments establish cross-domain generalization in controlled tasks, but do not identify the neural mechanism or specify exactly which computational features are shared across every domain. Complexity was operationalized using carefully selected laboratory stimuli, so its relation to the rich and context-dependent complexity of everyday information remains to be established.

## Key Takeaways

- Eleven experiments tested complexity across six stimulus domains.
- Reward associations for complexity generalized between different kinds of stimuli.
- The transfer influenced some task-irrelevant judgments.
- Individual preferences for simplicity were correlated across domains.
- The findings support a domain-general representation of complexity.
