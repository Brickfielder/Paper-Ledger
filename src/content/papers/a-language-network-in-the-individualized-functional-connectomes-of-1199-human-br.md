## Summary
This study asked whether a language-selective brain network can be recovered from individualized functional connectomes without using a language localizer task or even knowing what task a person is doing. The authors analyzed 1,957 fMRI sessions from 1,199 unique participants collected in the Fedorenko lab between 2007 and 2024, excluding people with brain lesions or known neurodevelopmental disorders. Their method used only low-frequency BOLD timecourses from a single scan session and did not regress out task structure, making the approach intentionally general and simple. They first built voxelwise connectivity profiles to an atlas, then repeatedly clustered voxels with k-means, aligned the repeated clusterings, and averaged them into probabilistic individualized network maps. Network labels were assigned by spatial similarity to reference atlases for 16 candidate functional networks. The main result was that this procedure consistently recovered a frontotemporal network they call LangFC. LangFC showed the expected language-network topography and was clearly separated from other functional networks in the same individuals. It was also selective for language-related contrasts, especially the widely used sentences-versus-nonwords localizer contrast. The network emerged across different task states, suggesting that it is not specific to resting-state data. The authors also report that LangFC differs functionally from nearby networks, is stable within individuals, and varies across individuals. The network was robust to changes in parcellation granularity and to alternative, qualitatively different matching procedures. In direct comparisons, the individualized connectivity-derived language network reproduced patterns seen with traditional task-based localizer methods. A key methodological point is that the analysis was designed to work from arbitrary fMRI timecourses alone, which strengthens claims about generality but also means the approach still depends on choices such as clustering, atlas matching, and the available scan session. Overall, the paper argues that individualized functional connectomics can recover a language network at much larger scale than previous studies and may be useful for both research and practical localization.
## Why This Matters
This work suggests that language-related brain organization can be identified from routine fMRI data without needing a dedicated language task. That could make language-network mapping more practical for large studies, retrospective datasets, and potentially clinical contexts.
## Caveats and Limitations
The evidence is strong in scale, but the analysis still depends on reference atlases and clustering decisions, so the result is not completely assumption-free. The data come from a single research environment and a selected participant pool, which may limit how broadly the findings generalize. Because the paper is largely methodological and comparative rather than experimental, it shows that the network can be recovered, but it does not by itself resolve broader debates about what computations the network supports.
## Key Takeaways
- Individualized functional connectivity can recover a language-selective network from arbitrary fMRI sessions.
- The sample is very large for this literature: 1,199 unique brains and 1,957 sessions.
- The recovered network matches prior language-localizer topography and task responses.
- The method works across task states and is robust to several analysis choices.
- The approach could make language-network localization easier in existing datasets and applied settings.
