## Summary

This narrative review examines how large language models are being used in medical research, focusing on systematic reviews, scientific writing and clinical studies. The authors reviewed literature published from 2023 to 2025 across major scholarly databases and preprint sources.

For evidence synthesis, the review describes promising performance for data extraction and reductions in screening workload, but much weaker agreement for risk-of-bias assessment. In scientific writing, it highlights fabricated references and demographic bias as recurring risks. For clinical research, it identifies possible roles in statistical coding and protocol development, provided researchers validate the output.

The review’s central argument is that these tools are useful but unstable. Their most appropriate role is as supervised assistants: they may reduce effort on bounded tasks, but researchers must retain critical thinking, check claims and take responsibility for methodological decisions.

## Why This Matters

Medical research contains many repetitive and text-heavy tasks, making it an obvious setting for language-model assistance. This review is valuable because it resists the idea that a generally capable model is automatically reliable in every research task. In particular, it separates work that can be streamlined from work that needs independent methodological judgement.

## Caveats and Limitations

- This is a narrative review, so its search and appraisal methods are less reproducible than those of a systematic review.
- The evidence spans different model generations, prompts, datasets and evaluation standards.
- Results such as extraction accuracy or workload reduction are task-specific and may not reproduce in a new setting.
- The review discusses risks including hallucinated references and bias; these need active safeguards rather than a generic disclaimer.
- The field is changing rapidly, so some examples may age quickly.

## Key Takeaways

- Large language models can assist medical-research workflows, particularly information extraction, screening, coding and drafting.
- Their outputs require verification, especially for citations, risk-of-bias judgements and clinical claims.
- Human-in-the-loop workflows preserve accountability and critical thinking.
- Local validation matters more than broad performance claims.
