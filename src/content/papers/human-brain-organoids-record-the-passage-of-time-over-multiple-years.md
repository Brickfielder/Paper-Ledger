## Summary
The authors cultured human cortical organoids for more than five years and analyzed them with single-cell transcriptomics, DNA methylation profiling, electron microscopy, and functional imaging. Their goal was to ask whether human brain organoids can keep developing over very long periods and whether they preserve the slow timing of human brain maturation. They profiled 110 organoids in total, spanning early development through five years in culture, and compared the organoid data with reference datasets from endogenous human cortex. Early organoids mapped to fetal developmental stages, while later organoids shifted toward late prenatal and postnatal-like states. The transcriptional data showed that major cell populations continued to progress through maturation programs over time. The epigenetic data showed similar long-term dynamics, with progressive DNA methylation changes followed by more stable patterns later in culture. Several methylation-based clocks also tracked culture duration, suggesting that organoids carry a time-dependent molecular history. The paper argues that organoids not only develop, but also preserve a kind of record of elapsed developmental time. The authors further report that progenitor cells in older organoids can generate later-born cell types without re-running the earliest developmental steps. They interpret this as evidence that organoids can “recall” developmental age after long culture. The study also examined cell-type survival and found that many expected cortical cell types were present across the time course, though not all populations persisted equally well. A key methodological strength is the integrated, single-cell, multi-omic approach over an unusually long culture period, which lets the authors track maturation at cell-type resolution rather than averaging whole organoids. The main caveat is that these are in vitro models, so they can only approximate parts of human brain development and may not capture full in vivo tissue organization or environmental inputs.
## Why This Matters
This work shows that human brain organoids can mature on a timescale of years, not just months, making them more useful for studying later stages of human neural development. It also suggests that developmental timing is encoded in molecular programs that persist in cultured tissue, which could help researchers study otherwise inaccessible periods of brain maturation.
## Caveats and Limitations
The evidence comes from organoids in culture, so it cannot fully substitute for the complexity of the human brain in the body. Long-term organoid studies also face selection effects, because some cell types survive better than others over years, which can bias what is observed. In addition, the findings show strong molecular correspondence with developmental timing, but they do not by themselves prove that all aspects of in vivo maturation or function are faithfully reproduced.
## Key Takeaways
- Long-term cortical organoids can continue developing for more than five years.
- Single-cell RNA-seq and DNA methylation together showed progressive maturation over time.
- Organoid molecular states increasingly resembled later prenatal and postnatal human cortex.
- Epigenetic clocks tracked culture duration, supporting a time-recording view of organoid development.
- The study used integrated, cell-type-resolved profiling, but culture models still have limits in capturing full brain complexity.
