## Summary
This study asked whether the structure of early experience changes how animals learn complex tasks and how neural dynamics reflect that learning. The authors focused on an odor-timing delayed non-match-to-sample task that had previously been used in mice, where correct behavior depends on distinguishing trial types defined by stimulus duration. They first trained recurrent neural networks on task variants that either included or omitted a shaping phase that withheld the no-go trial type until later. They then compared those model predictions with behavioral and electrophysiological recordings from mice trained with matched staged curricula. In both the RNNs and mice, the absence of well-structured early experience led to rigid strategies and repeated errors. By contrast, balanced early training supported better generalization to the full task. The model analysis showed that shaped networks learned lower-dimensional, more organized dynamics rather than tangled trajectories. These shaped dynamics were associated with task representations that better reflected the temporal structure of the problem. When the full task was introduced, the shaped networks needed only a small adjustment to inhibit responses on no-go trials. Networks without shaping, or those given remedial curricula later, tended to collapse onto single fixed-point-like solutions that did not generalize well. The mouse data were consistent with the model predictions, with the medial entorhinal cortex showing activity patterns that more closely matched the structured representations seen in the trained networks. The authors argue that the curriculum itself, not just the amount of prior experience, determines whether flexible abstractions emerge. Methodologically, a key strength is the paired RNN-and-mouse design, which allowed the authors to connect learning behavior to circuit-level dynamics. A key limitation is that the mechanistic claims are strongest for this one task family, so it remains unclear how broadly they generalize across other forms of learning.
## Why This Matters
This work shows that early training can do more than teach a task step by step; it can shape the kind of strategy an animal later uses. In practical terms, that means the order and structure of experience may matter as much as the experience itself when designing training protocols or interpreting learning failures.
## Caveats and Limitations
The conclusions come from a specific odor-timing paradigm, so the generality of the findings to other cognitive domains, species, or brain areas is not yet established. The RNN analysis is helpful for building intuition, but it is still a simplified model, and the link between the observed medial entorhinal dynamics and causal computation remains correlational rather than definitive. 
## Key Takeaways
- Structured early experience promoted flexible generalization in both RNNs and mice.
- Poorly structured or delayed training produced rigid strategies and repeated errors.
- Shaping encouraged lower-dimensional, more organized neural dynamics in the model.
- The medial entorhinal cortex showed activity patterns consistent with the model's structured representations.
- Curriculum design mattered, not just the fact that prior experience existed.
