## Summary

This review uses **perfusion impairment** to mean the failure of an artificial perfusate to distribute uniformly through an ischemic brain. The authors argue that it begins within minutes after circulatory arrest and is spatially heterogeneous: some regions may receive fluid while others remain poorly reached.

They organise the problem into four interacting mechanisms:

- **Intravascular obstruction:** agonal thrombi, postmortem red-cell aggregation, and possibly smaller obstructions restrict flow.
- **Perivascular cellular edema:** ischemia-driven swelling of endothelial cells and nearby astrocytes compresses capillary lumens.
- **Mural-cell contraction:** vascular smooth-muscle cells, precapillary sphincters and pericytes narrow the microvasculature.
- **Vessel-wall breakdown:** a degraded blood–brain barrier lets perfusate leak into parenchyma, worsening edema and reducing downstream delivery.

The review then compares intervention classes. Anticoagulants and fibrinolytics may be more useful before or around circulatory arrest than when added after ischemia is established; the latter may be too late for many postmortem clots, which are not fibrin-rich. Vasodilators have mixed evidence and may require active cellular metabolism. A dedicated blood washout has intuitive appeal, but its net benefit before fixation is uncertain and it can consume time or worsen edema.

The authors identify surfactants, osmotic agents and colloids as plausible ways to improve capillary flow or reduce edema, but each brings its own trade-offs. In particular, ionic surfactants can damage membranes, and more viscous colloids can reduce flow at a given pressure. Higher perfusion pressure can overcome resistance, yet may cause vessel damage and extravasation; hypothermia slows ischemic injury and autolysis but can also promote edema during perfusion. Other proposed approaches—including lipophilic perfusates, retrograde venous perfusion, decompressive craniectomy and sonothrombolysis—remain context-specific or largely untested for brain banking.

The central conclusion is not that one intervention is ready for use. It is that a specimen-specific combination, informed by real-time regional-flow and flow-pressure measurements, will likely be needed—and that tissue preservation quality, rather than vascular filling alone, is the relevant endpoint.

## Why This Matters

For large-scale human connectomics, a brain is only as useful as the least well-preserved regions needed to trace circuits through it. The review makes clear why a visually successful perfusion or a reasonable aggregate flow rate can conceal regional preservation failures.

It also usefully separates two goals that can come into tension:

- **Improving delivery:** clear obstructions, raise flow and keep the perfusate intravascular.
- **Preserving the target tissue:** avoid edema, membrane disruption, osmotic stress and pressure-related ultrastructural damage.

That distinction gives preservation research a sharper experimental agenda. A candidate protocol needs direct tests of regional coverage and electron-microscopy-scale integrity, rather than relying only on vascular patency or outcomes borrowed from resuscitation and transplantation.

## Caveats and Limitations

- This is a version-1 preprint and has not been peer reviewed.
- It is a narrative synthesis. Its literature-search approach is useful for mapping intervention classes, but it does not provide the predefined inclusion criteria, risk-of-bias assessment or quantitative effect estimates of a systematic review.
- The evidence base is highly indirect for its main application. Findings from animal reperfusion, organ transplantation, forensic imaging and embalming may not transfer to ex-vivo human brain fixation or cryopreservation.
- The proposed interventions target different biological states and time windows. Results from living, metabolically supported reperfusion should not be treated as evidence that the same agent will work during rapid acellular preservation.
- Several outcomes are incomplete proxies for connectomic utility. Better flow, less retained blood or greater vascular filling may still coexist with damage to membranes, extracellular space or synaptic ultrastructure.
- The relative importance of the four mechanisms varies across specimens and ischemia durations, but is not yet well quantified. Aggregate pressure and flow measurements can hide this regional heterogeneity.
- The authors disclose roles in organisations working on brain preservation, biostasis and tissue preservation. That does not invalidate the review, but it makes independent replication and direct protocol comparisons especially valuable.
- This paper is about research-tissue preservation and related vascular physiology. It is not clinical guidance for treating stroke, cardiac arrest or brain injury.

## Key Takeaways

- Perfusion impairment after ischemia is multifactorial; no single drug or mechanical setting is likely to solve it across specimens.
- Reducing the interval from circulatory arrest to preservation is the most direct way to limit the problem before additional interventions are considered.
- Anticoagulants, fibrinolytics and vasodilators have important timing and metabolic constraints; they should not be assumed to work when introduced only after substantial ischemia.
- Pressure, temperature, surfactants and osmotic/colloid composition must be treated as coupled variables because a gain in flow can produce edema or structural damage.
- Future protocols should measure regional delivery and preservation quality directly, then test whether any gains support the connectomic endpoint rather than merely improving vascular appearance.
