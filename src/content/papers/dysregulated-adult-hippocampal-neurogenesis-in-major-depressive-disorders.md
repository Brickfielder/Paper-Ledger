## Summary
The study used a multimodal human hippocampus atlas to examine whether adult hippocampal neurogenesis is altered in major depressive disorder (MDD). The authors combined single-nucleus RNA sequencing, single-nucleus ATAC sequencing, spatial transcriptomics, and regional proteomics from nonmedicated individuals with MDD and neurotypical controls. They analyzed about 500,000 nuclei from dentate gyrus and cornu ammonis regions and integrated the data to annotate major hippocampal cell types and spatial locations. This atlas identified a putative adult neurogenic lineage in the human hippocampus, including stem-like, progenitor, neuroblast, and immature granule cell states. Trajectory analyses suggested a developmental progression from neural stem cells through intermediate progenitors and neuroblasts toward immature granule cells. The analysis also indicated a second branch in which some cells retained or re-expressed immature markers later in the trajectory, consistent with possible dematuration or a less mature cell state. Chromatin accessibility and transcription-factor footprinting showed stage-specific regulatory programs across the lineage, with stemness, differentiation, and maturation-associated factors changing in an ordered way. Spatial transcriptomics placed neurogenic-like states in the subgranular zone and related immature granule-cell signatures in the granule cell layer, supporting an anatomic link to the dentate gyrus niche. Xenium in situ data further validated expression of neurogenic markers in these regions and showed that some immature markers were detectable in cells located in the granule cell layer. Comparing MDD with control samples, the authors report a shift in cell-state distribution consistent with stalled neurogenesis in MDD. They also describe broader dysregulation in the hippocampal memory circuit, including altered excitatory-inhibitory balance, synaptic plasticity, neurotransmission, long noncoding RNAs, intracellular trafficking, stress-related pathways, serotonergic and glutamatergic signaling, and neuroinflammation. The strongest methodological point is the use of multiple orthogonal human tissue assays and spatial validation rather than relying on a single sequencing layer. The main limitation is that the work is observational and based on postmortem tissue, so it cannot prove that the inferred neurogenic changes cause depression or result from it.
## Why This Matters
This paper provides one of the most detailed human hippocampus maps to date and ties it to a long-standing question in depression research: whether adult neurogenesis is altered in people with MDD. If the findings hold up, they could help explain memory and mood symptoms in depression and point to new biological pathways for study.
## Caveats and Limitations
The evidence is correlative, so it cannot establish causality between dysregulated neurogenesis and depressive symptoms. Postmortem tissue also captures a single time point and may be influenced by illness history, agonal factors, and other unmeasured variables, even though the study focused on nonmedicated participants. In addition, the putative neurogenic lineage is inferred from molecular signatures and trajectory methods, which are informative but not a direct lineage-tracing demonstration.
## Key Takeaways
- Multimodal profiling found a putative adult neurogenic lineage in the human hippocampus.
- MDD samples showed patterns consistent with stalled or dysregulated neurogenesis.
- Spatial and in situ data supported localization of neurogenic states to the dentate gyrus niche.
- The hippocampal memory circuit showed broader transcriptional and epigenetic dysregulation.
- The study is strong on human tissue integration but remains observational, not causal.
