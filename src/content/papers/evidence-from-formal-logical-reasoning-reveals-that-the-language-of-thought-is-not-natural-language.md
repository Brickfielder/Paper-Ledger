## Summary

This preprint asks whether formal logical reasoning uses the same linguistic representations that support sentence comprehension and production. It combines two complementary approaches:

- **Study 1 - fMRI:** healthy adults completed inductive rule-discovery, deductive syllogistic, and matrix-reasoning tasks while the authors measured responses in individually localised language and multiple-demand (MD) networks.
- **Study 2 - neuropsychology:** two men with profound, agrammatic aphasia after extensive left peri-Sylvian damage completed non-verbal inductive and matrix-reasoning tasks.

The language network responded robustly to its independent language localiser, but showed little or no increase for the critical reasoning-demand contrasts. The induction contrast produced a small effect, but it was over four times weaker than the language localiser effect; the deductive syllogism and matrix-reasoning contrasts were not significant in the language network. This is not because the logic tasks were easy: harder conditions impaired accuracy and slowed responses, and reasoning-related activity appeared elsewhere.

Inductive rule discovery and matrix reasoning strongly recruited the domain-general MD network. In contrast, the hard-versus-easy syllogism contrast did not recruit MD regions; a whole-brain analysis instead identified a distinct frontal-parietal set of regions responsive to deductive complexity. The results therefore suggest that formal reasoning is not a single process and does not simply reuse the language system.

The aphasia evidence provides a causal complement. Despite severe grammatical comprehension and production difficulties, the two participants solved most inductive rules (19/25 and 39/40) and performed well above age-matched norms on WASI-II matrix reasoning (25/30 and 26/30; T-scores 73 and 68). They did not complete the verbal syllogism task, because its language demands make it unsuitable for people with profound aphasia.

## Why This Matters

The study is a strong challenge to the idea that people literally think in natural-language words and syntax when performing formal inference. Language can be necessary to communicate a syllogism or parse its sentence form, but the authors argue that the relational structure can then be passed to a distinct reasoning system.

It also makes a useful distinction between **language being involved in learning, communicating or offloading thought** and **language being the representational medium of thought itself**. The evidence supports the latter dissociation in adults, while leaving the former roles open.

## Caveats and Limitations

- This is a bioRxiv preprint and has not yet been peer reviewed.
- The most direct causal evidence comes from only two participants with profound aphasia. Their dissociation is informative, but it cannot characterise the full clinical variability of aphasia or lesion profiles.
- Because the syllogistic task was verbal, the aphasia study could test induction and matrix reasoning but not a purely deductive syllogism task. A non-verbal deductive task in a larger patient sample would strengthen the claim.
- The fMRI samples were modest and split across tasks (17 participants for induction and matrix reasoning; 23 for syllogisms). A null difficulty effect in language regions is not, on its own, proof of zero contribution; the lesion evidence makes the overall inference stronger.
- The paradigms are deliberately formal and decontextualised. They do not settle whether language can support pragmatic, social, culturally learned or everyday reasoning.
- Matrix reasoning combines inductive and deductive operations, so it is not a pure measure of either one.
- The study does not identify the positive representational format of a language of thought. Candidates discussed include visuo-spatial models, domain-specific schemas and abstract symbolic/probabilistic representations.
- The authors explicitly do not address whether language is important for learning logical concepts in childhood or for developing reasoning skills.

## Key Takeaways

- Natural-language brain regions appear to parse verbal logic problems without doing the incremental inferential work required to solve them.
- Formal reasoning is neurally heterogeneous: induction and matrix reasoning align with MD activity, whereas syllogistic deduction recruits a different set of regions.
- Severe loss of grammatical language need not abolish non-verbal logical reasoning in the mature brain.
- The results argue against natural language as the universal medium of mature logical thought, not against language as a useful tool for communication, learning or cognitive offloading.
