## Summary

This study builds a cross-species single-cell atlas of the striatum, a basal-ganglia structure important for movement, motivation, reward and decision-making. The researchers analysed single nuclei from 109 human and 22 mouse samples spanning dorsal and ventral striatum, then used spatial methods to validate the resulting maps.

The atlas identifies rare neuronal populations and continuous transcriptional gradients along the dorsolateral-to-ventromedial axis. Comparing species revealed important differences in striatal cell types and regional specialisation, with potential consequences for pharmacological targets and the interpretation of rodent models.

The authors integrate the atlas with genetic and pharmacological evidence. They report human-enriched sites of opioid-receptor expression and a ventral bias in the effects of chronic antipsychotic exposure. They also combine single-cell transcriptomics with measurement of somatic trinucleotide-repeat expansion to examine Huntington's disease, identifying differences in vulnerability across striatal subregions and neuronal subtypes.

## Why This Matters

A cell-resolved map can help bridge molecular findings, clinical disease associations and the circuitry of the striatum. It is particularly valuable where disease risk or drug action is being inferred from animal models: the study shows that relevant cell types and expression patterns are not simply interchangeable between humans and rodents.

## Caveats and Limitations

- The human analyses are associative, not experimental tests of disease causation.
- Tissue sampling and nucleus-based sequencing cannot capture every aspect of living-cell physiology or circuit activity.
- Cross-species comparisons require care: mouse data are informative but are not a direct proxy for human molecular organisation.
- The Huntington's disease findings identify differential vulnerability rather than a complete causal pathway.
- Pharmacological and genetic integrations are useful for prioritising hypotheses, but need functional follow-up.

## Key Takeaways

- A large human-and-mouse single-nucleus atlas maps striatal cell types and subregional specialisation.
- Human and rodent striatum differ in ways that matter for drug targets and disease mechanisms.
- The atlas connects cell types to addiction risk and antipsychotic-related biology.
- Huntington's disease vulnerability varies by striatal subregion and neuronal subtype.
- The resource can guide more precise studies of basal-ganglia disorders.
