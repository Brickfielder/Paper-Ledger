## Summary

Humans and chimpanzees share a recent common ancestor, but their genomes have changed along separate evolutionary paths. This review surveys genetic differences between the two species and asks how they might contribute to differences in gene expression, proteins, cellular biology, anatomy and cognition. It emphasizes that there is no single “human gene” that explains human identity. The relevant differences are spread across many kinds of DNA changes and biological processes.

The authors describe several broad classes of genomic difference:

- **Chromosome structure:** Humans have 46 chromosomes and chimpanzees 48. Human chromosome 2 formed when two ancestral chromosomes fused. The species also differ in several chromosome inversions and other structural features.
- **Single-letter changes:** The review cites an estimated 1.23% difference in aligned single-nucleotide positions, while noting that insertions, deletions and larger structural changes add substantially to the total genomic differences.
- **Insertions, deletions and copy-number changes:** DNA segments can be lost, inserted or duplicated. These changes may alter gene dosage, remove regulatory sequences, or affect gene structure. Examples discussed include the human-specific truncated SRGAP2 copies, which have been proposed to influence neuronal development.
- **Mobile genetic elements:** Transposable elements such as Alu, LINE-1 and SVA can insert into new locations, rearrange DNA, or provide regulatory sequences. Their activity has contributed to lineage-specific changes in both genomes.
- **Protein-coding differences:** Many proteins are very similar between the species, but some genes have accumulated substitutions or changed function. The review discusses FOXP2, genes related to brain development, immunity and cell-surface biology, among other examples.
- **Non-coding regulatory regions:** Much of the DNA associated with human-accelerated regions does not code for proteins. Such regions may affect gene regulation, including genes involved in transcription and neuronal development.

The article’s main biological argument is that changes in **gene regulation** may have broad effects. A DNA change can influence the timing, location or amount of gene activity, which may alter networks of interacting genes even when the protein-coding sequence itself remains similar. The authors assemble examples where particular genetic differences have been proposed to affect neural development or other traits. These examples are candidates and possible mechanisms, rather than proof of a direct route from one variant to a complex human characteristic.

The review also notes that genome comparisons are affected by the quality and completeness of reference assemblies. Earlier ape genomes contained gaps and were sometimes assembled or annotated using the human genome as a guide, potentially obscuring lineage-specific variants. More complete, independently assembled genomes and broader sampling of population diversity are needed to distinguish fixed species differences from variation within humans or chimpanzees.

## Why This Matters

The paper offers a broad map of the kinds of genetic changes that can accumulate during evolution. It cautions against explaining complex traits through a simple list of uniquely human genes: regulatory changes, structural variants, mobile DNA and interactions among many genes may all matter.

For cognition and brain evolution, the review is useful as a catalogue of candidate pathways and a reminder that genomic association is only an early step. Showing that a sequence differs between species does not by itself show that it causes a particular behavioural or cognitive difference. That requires functional experiments and evidence connecting the variant to changes in cells, circuits and ultimately behaviour.

## Caveats and Limitations

- This is a narrative review of research available by 2020; it does not report a new experiment or a systematic search and appraisal of all evidence.
- Genomic estimates and examples reflect the assemblies and annotation methods available at publication. Newer long-read and pangenome resources may revise what is considered species-specific.
- Many proposed links between variants and traits are indirect. The review’s discussion of possible cognitive or anatomical consequences should not be read as proof of causation.
- Complex traits arise from many interacting genes, developmental processes and environments. A species difference in one gene cannot, by itself, explain a broad trait such as language or intelligence.
- Differences within each species matter. A variant found in a reference genome is not necessarily fixed across all humans or all chimpanzees.
- The reported percentages depend on what is compared and how: aligned single-letter substitutions do not capture all insertions, deletions, duplications and structural rearrangements.

## Key Takeaways

- Human–chimpanzee genomic differences include point mutations, structural changes, gene copy-number variation, mobile elements and regulatory DNA.
- Protein-coding sequences are highly similar, while non-coding and regulatory changes may affect when and where genes are expressed.
- The review presents candidate links to neural and other traits, but these are not established causal explanations for human cognition.
- Genome quality and within-species diversity are essential when interpreting claims about “human-specific” changes.
- The paper is best used as a 2020 overview of hypotheses and mechanisms, alongside newer comparative-genomics evidence.
