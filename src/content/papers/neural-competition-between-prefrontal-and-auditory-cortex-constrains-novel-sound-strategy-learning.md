## Summary

Animals often have more than one way to reach the same reward. A familiar strategy may work reasonably well, while a new cue could support a faster or more reliable alternative. This study asks how the brain switches between those strategies.

The authors trained female mice in an ethological T-maze pup-retrieval task. At first, mice used a predisposed **win-stay** rule: search the arm where they found a pup on the preceding trial. A novel amplitude-modulated sound, played from the arm that would contain the pup, offered a more reliable guide. Over eight days, the mice gradually shifted from the memory-based default to following the sound.

Recordings revealed distinct neural signatures. In auditory cortex (ACx), trial-by-trial activity during maze search predicted whether the mouse would correctly follow the sound, even on the first day. This prognostic signal grew as performance improved and was often expressed as lower spiking on correct trials. In mPFC, neural activity instead reflected the fading win-stay strategy rather than the emerging auditory strategy.

Causal perturbations produced the key dissociation. Silencing ACx slowed learning of sound-guided search. In contrast, silencing mPFC accelerated it: mice adopted the auditory strategy sooner, then returned toward normal performance when mPFC function was restored. The authors compared computational accounts and found that a model of dynamic competition between decentralised strategy experts captured these effects better than an auditory-learning model alone.

Their interpretation is deliberately counterintuitive: in this task, mPFC does not principally enable flexible switching. It helps implement a prior-based default strategy that is adaptive in familiar conditions but competes with a newly acquired, more efficient rule.

## Why This Matters

The paper separates *learning a cue* from *letting that cue control behaviour*. A mouse may acquire useful auditory information while an existing strategy still wins the competition for action. That distinction helps explain why effective new strategies can be slow to express in natural decision-making.

It also challenges a simple prefrontal-flexibility story. Prefrontal systems may sometimes preserve prior knowledge and stabilise useful defaults, with the cost that they impede changes when the environment makes another source of evidence more informative.

## Caveats and Limitations

- The task compares one persistent artificial sound cue with one spatial win-stay strategy in female mice; it does not establish a general role for mPFC across all forms of flexibility.
- Chemogenetic silencing is spatially broad and temporally coarse. It does not identify the precise cell types, projections or moment-by-moment computations responsible.
- The sound remained available until the correct arm was reached and rewards were reliable. The authors note that mPFC may be more necessary for short-lived cues, working-memory demands or uncertain contingencies.
- The multiexpert competition model is strongly supported relative to the alternatives tested, but it is still an explanatory model that needs testing in other behaviours and neural systems.

## Key Takeaways

- A default win-stay strategy and a learned auditory strategy can compete for control of the same decision.
- Auditory cortex contributes to acquiring and expressing sound-guided search.
- In this task, mPFC maintains the prior-based strategy and thereby restrains faster adoption of the new one.
- Removing mPFC can improve expression of the better strategy, even though it does not make the cue-learning system intrinsically stronger.
- Adaptive learning may depend on resolving competition among specialised neural systems, not just reinforcing a single policy.
