## Summary
This Perspective argues that generative AI should not be treated as just another classroom tool, because it changes the cognitive and social organization of schooling itself. The authors frame schools as cognitive ecologies, meaning that learning emerges from interactions among students, teachers, tools, routines, and institutional norms. They place GenAI in this broader setting rather than evaluating it only by whether it improves individual test scores or convenience. A central claim is that GenAI is unusually active, persistent, and generalist compared with most earlier educational technologies. It can combine functions such as explanation, feedback, tutoring, assessment support, and content generation within a single interface. That consolidation may redistribute epistemic labor away from learners and teachers and toward the system. The paper argues that this shift is happening inside school systems that already emphasize efficiency, standardization, and control. The authors identify a pedagogical gap between how learning science understands development and how AI systems are typically designed. They also identify a goal gap between what schools can easily measure and the broader developmental aims of education. In their view, GenAI does not create these problems from scratch, but can intensify pre-existing mismatches in schooling. The article also notes that current evidence on educational AI is still preliminary, especially regarding whether heavy use supports learning or erodes metacognitive capacities such as critical thinking and epistemic ownership. Methodologically, this is a conceptual and theoretical Perspective rather than an empirical study, so its claims are based on synthesis and critique rather than new data. The authors end by proposing that GenAI be used as a diagnostic opportunity to rethink schooling around embodied, collaborative, and distinctly human forms of learning.
## Why This Matters
This paper matters because it shifts the discussion from “Can AI help students do school tasks faster?” to “What kind of schooling are we building around AI?” That is a useful reframing for teachers, policymakers, and researchers deciding whether GenAI should be inserted into existing systems or used to rethink them.
## Caveats and Limitations
Because this is a Perspective, it does not present original experiments or direct evidence that its proposed harms or benefits occur in particular classrooms. Some of the concerns are forward-looking and depend on assumptions about how schools will adopt GenAI in practice. The argument is strong conceptually, but its generalization to different educational levels, subjects, and institutional contexts still needs empirical testing.
## Key Takeaways
- GenAI is presented as a change to the structure of schooling, not just a productivity aid.
- The authors use a 4E/cognitive-ecology lens to show that learning is shaped by tools and institutions.
- Two main problems are highlighted: a pedagogical gap and a goal gap.
- GenAI may consolidate multiple teaching functions into one system, changing who does epistemic work.
- The paper recommends redesigning education around embodied, collaborative learning rather than only adapting old routines to new tools.
