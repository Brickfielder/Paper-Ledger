## Summary
Approach–avoidance conflict occurs when an option offers both potential reward and potential punishment, creating a difficult choice. To study the human orbitofrontal cortex (OFC) during this kind of decision, the authors recorded intracranial activity from six patients implanted with stereotactic EEG electrodes for clinical reasons. Participants performed a gamified corridor task in which they chose whether to approach or avoid offers that varied in reward and punishment magnitude and had fixed, known probabilities. The behavioral data showed that decisions were sensitive to subjective value, with slower choices and greater conflict near intermediate-value offers. The best-fitting behavioral model was a prospect-theory-style subjective value model rather than simpler alternatives. Using high-frequency activity as a proxy for local neural population firing, the authors examined how signals varied across the medial-to-lateral OFC axis. They found opposite pre-decision dynamics in different OFC subregions: activity tended to increase in medial OFC near the medial orbital sulcus, but decrease in lateral OFC. These signals were aligned with the upcoming choice, with medial sites showing higher activity before approach and lateral sites showing lower activity before approach. The authors also showed that the pattern was related to electrode position along the medial–lateral axis, supporting an anatomical gradient rather than a diffuse OFC effect. Importantly, they used variance partitioning and an offer-balanced analysis to show that the decision-related signal was not explained only by subjective value or by differences in offer identity. A residual regression analysis further suggested that decision encoding remained after accounting for value-related variance. Spatial clustering identified a medial hotspot for positive approach-related activity and a lateral hotspot for negative approach-related activity. Lower-frequency bands showed some decision-related effects, but these did not track electrode location as clearly as high-frequency activity. Overall, the study argues that human OFC contains distinct medial and lateral contributions to approach–avoidance decisions, captured with high temporal resolution intracranial recordings.
## Why This Matters
This work helps pin down where and how the human brain resolves decisions that involve both temptation and risk. It also shows that the OFC is not just a single value center, but may contain functionally different subregions that push choices in opposite directions.
## Caveats and Limitations
The study is based on only six neurosurgical patients, so the findings may not generalize broadly. Because the task is artificial and the recordings come from a clinically selected population, the results should be interpreted as strong mechanistic evidence but not as a complete picture of everyday decision-making. The analyses are careful about separating choice from value, but intracranial recordings still support correlation rather than direct causal claims.
## Key Takeaways
- Intracranial recordings revealed distinct medial and lateral OFC signals before approach–avoidance choices.
- Medial OFC showed increased high-frequency activity before approach, while lateral OFC showed decreased activity.
- Behavior was well described by a prospect-theory-style subjective value model.
- The decision-related OFC effects persisted after controls for subjective value and offer identity.
- The study suggests a functional gradient across human OFC rather than a single uniform decision signal.
