title: "Predicting follow-up cognition using estimated structural and functional connectivity networks and artificial intelligence in multiple sclerosis"
summary: >-
  Tozlu and colleagues tested whether structural and functional connectomes estimated from routine lesion MRI could help explain and predict cognition in multiple sclerosis (MS). In 171 people with MS, they used lesion masks and the Network Modification (NeMo) tool to estimate structural connectivity (eSC), then used the Krakencoder to estimate functional connectivity (eFC) from eSC. Regional connectome measures were combined with demographic, clinical, conventional MRI and baseline cognitive variables in ridge-regression models, evaluated with repeated five-fold cross-validation. Regional eSC and eFC models showed moderate prediction, with their strongest performance for follow-up SDMT; in the main results the median correlation was 0.54 for eSC and 0.49 for eFC. Higher or lower estimated connectivity in particular cerebellar and attention-network regions was associated with follow-up processing speed. Adding eSC and eFC together did not reliably improve on either alone. The authors’ aim was primarily to map lesion-related circuits associated with cognition, rather than to produce the best possible clinical prediction model.

## Summary

People with multiple sclerosis can have different cognitive outcomes despite apparently similar measures of lesion burden or tissue loss. The authors ask whether maps of brain connectivity estimated from routine structural MRI lesion masks can reveal circuit-level associations with cognition and help predict cognitive scores over time.

The study included 171 people with MS. The researchers used lesion masks from clinical MRI and the Network Modification (NeMo) tool to estimate structural connectivity (eSC), representing pathways likely disrupted by the lesions. They then supplied eSC to the Krakencoder, a model trained to map between structural and functional connectomes, to estimate functional connectivity (eFC). They summarized these estimated connectomes regionally and used ridge regression to predict scores on three Brief International Cognitive Assessment for MS (BICAMS) tests: processing speed (SDMT), verbal learning and memory (CVLT), and visuospatial memory (BVMT). Scores were available at baseline and around four years later. Models were assessed with repeated five-fold cross-validation.

The regional eSC and eFC models achieved their strongest results for four-year follow-up SDMT. In the reported main analysis, the median correlation between predicted and observed follow-up SDMT was 0.54 for eSC and 0.49 for eFC. The estimated structural and functional connectomes together generally did not improve prediction over either set alone. Regional estimates performed better than pairwise connection models.

The circuit associations were not uniform across cognitive domains. The models identified cerebellar and dorsal-attention-network measures among those associated with follow-up processing speed. In particular, lower estimated connectivity in some cerebellar and attention-network regions was associated with lower follow-up SDMT. Functional estimates in the default-mode network also showed associations with performance, although the direction and interpretation of these patterns are not straightforward: they could reflect compensation, maladaptation, or other factors.

The comparison models are important for interpreting the result. Models using demographics, clinical information and conventional MRI measures, but not estimated connectomes, generally predicted cognition better; for follow-up SDMT their median correlation was about 0.71. Baseline cognitive performance was also a particularly strong predictor of follow-up scores. The authors therefore frame eSC and eFC chiefly as tools for mapping lesion-related circuits associated with cognition, not as a superior stand-alone prediction method.

## Why This Matters

The approach may let researchers study brain-network correlates of cognition in existing clinical datasets that contain lesion MRI but lack diffusion MRI or functional MRI. That could widen access to connectome-informed research and help identify candidate circuits for future studies of cognitive rehabilitation or non-invasive stimulation.

The results also illustrate a useful distinction: clinical prediction may be strongest with a compact set of established measures, while a higher-dimensional connectome can still offer spatial detail about which networks are associated with performance. The paper provides an example of how to use inferred networks as a research lens while being cautious about interpreting them as direct measurements.

## Caveats and Limitations

- The sample was modest (171 people), relatively young, and predominantly relapsing-remitting MS; most participants had little cognitive impairment at baseline.
- The four-year follow-up cognitive sample was smaller than the enrolled cohort, with 111 participants contributing follow-up SDMT data.
- The models were internally cross-validated but not tested in an independent external cohort.
- eSC and eFC are inferred from lesion masks and reference data. They are not equivalent to patient-specific diffusion or functional MRI connectomes.
- The Krakencoder was trained using healthy young-adult data. In a separate MS dataset, eFC showed very weak correspondence with observed FC, which limits strong functional interpretations.
- Follow-up performance partly reflects baseline cognition, which was included as a predictor. The results do not show that connectivity estimates alone forecast future decline.
- Associations between estimated regional connectivity and cognitive scores are not causal evidence that those circuits produce the observed differences.
- The study population's generally stable or slightly improved test scores limit conclusions about prediction of progressive cognitive decline.

## Key Takeaways

- Lesion masks from routine MRI were used to estimate structural connectivity, and a second model inferred functional connectivity from those structural estimates.
- In this cohort, estimated-connectome models showed moderate associations with cognitive scores, with the strongest results for four-year SDMT.
- Regional cerebellar and attention-network estimates were among the measures associated with processing speed.
- Simpler models using clinical, demographic and conventional MRI variables predicted follow-up SDMT better than the connectome models.
- The main value proposed by the authors is circuit-level mapping in datasets that lack advanced connectivity imaging, not improved clinical forecasting.
