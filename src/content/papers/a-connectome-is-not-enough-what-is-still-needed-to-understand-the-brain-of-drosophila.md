## Summary

Scheffer and Meinertzhagen ask a deliberately demanding question: what would count as *understanding* the brain of a fruit fly? Their answer is functional and predictive. A model should be able to anticipate what happens when identified neurons or cell types are activated, silenced or restored—not merely reproduce a static anatomical description.

They therefore treat the Drosophila connectome as an essential starting point, not an endpoint. Electron-microscopy reconstruction can reveal neurons and chemical synapses, but it does not by itself tell us the neurotransmitter and receptor at a synapse, its sign, gain or time constant, the effects of neuromodulators, or how neural state changes with development, experience and context.

The commentary groups the missing work into several interdependent layers:

- **Molecular and biochemical identity:** transmitters, receptors, neuropeptides, amines and gap junctions, including where they act and how their effects combine.
- **Cell and circuit physiology:** neuronal and glial properties, synaptic plasticity, local field effects, and the mapping between morphological cell types, genetic access and gene-expression profiles.
- **Whole-animal function:** sensory feedback, muscles, biomechanics and aerodynamics; natural behaviour; circadian state; sex differences; and variation between individual animals.
- **Computation and infrastructure:** reduced but accurate neuron models, simulators that handle spiking and graded signalling, coupling to body simulations, and machine-readable integration of diverse data sources.

The authors argue that the key bottlenecks are not simply collecting a larger wiring map. They are integrating heterogeneous evidence in a shared computational framework, and characterising the precise and interacting effects of neuromodulators and hormones.

## Why This Matters

The paper offers a useful guardrail against treating a connectome as a finished explanatory model. A connection catalogue alone cannot specify what information is transmitted, how strongly, in what direction, over what time scale, or how the same circuit behaves in different internal and environmental states.

It also turns the broad ambition of “understanding a brain” into a practical standard: a model should make successful perturbation predictions and connect circuit activity to behaviour in an embodied animal. This reframes future progress as systems integration, rather than a race to complete ever-larger anatomical datasets.

Drosophila is the authors’ proving ground because its brain is small enough for near-complete reconstruction yet supports varied behaviour and unusually powerful genetic experiments. The same conceptual gap—between wiring and mechanism—scales to other connectomics programmes.

## Caveats and Limitations

- This is a Commentary, not a systematic evidence synthesis or a new experimental dataset.
- The paper is intentionally broad; it identifies major missing layers but does not rank them using formal evidence, costs or feasibility criteria.
- It reflects the state of the field in 2021. Several fly connectome datasets and analysis tools have advanced since then.
- The proposed criterion of understanding—predicting perturbation outcomes—is powerful but not the only philosophical or scientific definition of explanation.
- Some requirements are specific to Drosophila, including its genetic toolkit, biomechanics and particular repertoire of behaviours; translation to larger mammalian brains is conceptual rather than straightforward.

## Key Takeaways

- A complete connectome is necessary for mechanistic brain science but is not sufficient for functional understanding.
- Synaptic wiring must be complemented by transmitter, receptor, neuromodulator and gap-junction information to determine effective circuit dynamics.
- Physiological state, learning, glia, individual variation, sex and circadian rhythms mean that there is no single timeless “circuit function” to read directly from anatomy.
- Predictive brain models must couple neural circuits to the body, sensory feedback and real behaviour.
- Progress depends on coordinated, machine-readable integration across connectomics, genetics, physiology, behaviour and simulation.
